Read This First

I gained 12 pounds in a single month, a year and a half after bariatric surgery.

I stood on the scale in my grandfather's bathroom, overseas, full of comfort food and something colder than regret, and I thought the exact sentence that might be in your head right now, or might be someday: it stopped working.

It hadn't. I've since taken back 8 of those 12 pounds, not with a crash diet, but with the exact approach this book walks you through. My wife Renata went from 220 pounds to 110, a loss of 110 pounds. I went from a heaviest of 340 down to 215, a loss of 125. That's 235 pounds between us, and more importantly, we've kept losing past the phase where most people quietly slide backward.

But here's the truth that made us write this: the surgery is a tool, and nobody hands you the full manual. The clearance process is confusing. The early months are a crash course in a body that stopped negotiating. And the years after, the part every other book skips, are where the whole thing is actually won or lost.

This is the manual we needed. All of it. The clearances, the liquid diet that landed on Thanksgiving, the food that took the elevator back up, the hair in the shower drain, the plateau that broke in ten days instead of ten years, and yes, the chapter we've told our kids to skip.

Wherever you are in this, day one or year three or still deciding, start here.

Chapter 1: A Note Before You Start

◆

Before anything else, let's get the boring but genuinely important part out of the way. This book is not written by a doctor, a dietitian, or anyone with a medical degree hanging on their wall. It's written by two people who had bariatric surgery, did the work, and lived to tell the story with a sense of humor mostly intact. Nothing in here is medical advice, and nothing in here should ever go against what your surgeon, your dietitian, or your care team tells you. If something in this book ever seems to disagree with your medical team, your medical team wins. Every time. No exceptions, no matter how funny the paragraph that says otherwise.

What this book actually is: two people telling you the truth about what this experience is really like, the parts nobody puts in the pamphlet, alongside what worked for us. Think of it as the conversation you'd have with a friend who already went through it, not a replacement for the conversation you should be having with your surgeon.

Here's the other thing worth saying up front, because it matters more than it sounds like it should: this book isn't just for people who are exactly where we are, sitting in year two and beyond, staring down a slow body recomposition phase that makes watching paint dry look thrilling. If that's you, you're in the right place. But if you're three days post-op and still on the liquid diet counting the hours, this book is for you too. If you're six months in and riding the honeymoon phase where the weight is falling off and life feels like a highlight reel, there's something here for you. If you're still in the clearance process, gathering your psychological and cardiac evaluations and wondering if you're making the right call, welcome. And if you haven't had surgery at all and are just trying to understand what you're about to sign up for, pull up a chair.

Every season of this journey comes with its own version of the same core problem: the tool works, but only if you know how to keep working it. What changes season to season isn't the principle. It's just which part of it is testing you at that particular moment. Early on, it's the liquid diet and the fear of the unknown. Later, it's the invisible plateau and the quiet work of body recomposition. Later still, it's a trip abroad, a stressful year, an old habit finding a new disguise. We've tried to write this so that wherever you are when you pick it up, something in here is speaking directly to that exact moment, not just to ours.

One last thing before we get into it. We are going to make jokes in this book. Some of them are going to be ridiculous on purpose. If a joke ever sounds like it might actually be real advice, it isn't, and we'll always come back around and say the real thing plainly right after. Humor is how we got through this. It is not a substitute for the facts, and it never will be.

And since every product you've ever bought comes with the same fine print, here's ours, in the same spirit: results may vary. Batteries not included. Some assembly required, and by assembly we mean roughly two years of showing up for yourself three times a week. This guide has not been evaluated by the FDA, your surgeon, or your mother-in-law. Do not operate this book while ignoring your medical team. Side effects of reading may include laughing at things you've cried about, and the occasional urge to buy a smaller pair of pants.

Joking aside, that first line is the one to actually keep: results genuinely do vary. Every body, every procedure, every recovery is different, which is exactly why your care team outranks this book on every page. Everything you're about to read is our story and our patterns, offered as company and as a map of one road already walked, never as a promise that your road looks identical.

One practical note on how to read this. The book is built in short chapters on purpose, most of them take just a few minutes. Reading straight through works and tells one continuous story. But if you're in the middle of something specific right now, a plateau, the clearance paperwork, a hard week, jump straight to that section and come back for the rest later. This book was designed to be used, not just finished in order.

Alright. Let's talk about why we're really here.

Chapter 2: How We Got Here

◆

I was a normal weight kid. That part surprises people who only know me now, or knew me for the last twenty years. My family moved to the US when I was seven, in the late eighties, and the weight itself didn't start climbing until a few years later, in my preteens. My actual fight with it started at eleven years old, and the yo-yo effect that would define the next three decades of my life started right around then too, though I didn't have that language for it at the time. I just knew I lost weight sometimes, and then I didn't, and then I lost it again.

Sports helped, for a while. I swam competitively in clubs, and from around seventeen to twenty, I played basketball for years with the same close group of friends. Outside of organized sports, it was pickup soccer and street basketball with whoever else was around. By sixteen I was 6'4", and the height did two jobs at once: it helped on the court and in the pool, and it hid weight extremely well. Even at my thinnest as a teenager, I was still technically overweight. Nobody looking at me would have guessed it, including me most days. A big frame is an excellent place to hide extra pounds. It's a much worse place to actually lose them, because nothing about how you look tells you the truth.

At twenty one, I moved out of my parents' house and decided, with zero medical advice and considerably more confidence than the plan deserved, to fix things myself. The plan was eating once a day. Grilled chicken tenders on a George Foreman grill, steamed broccoli, repeat. I lost 80 pounds in six months. I kept most of it off for about a year, which is right around when I met Renata, and right around when we got married.

Here's the part that isn't unique to me, but is worth saying plainly anyway: getting married quietly killed the routine that had gotten me there. Going to the gym turned into going on dates. Dates involve food. Calories crept back in a few at a time, slowly enough that I didn't notice the trend until it wasn't a trend anymore, it was just where I lived. Within a couple of years, I was at a new heaviest.

The yo-yo diets kept coming after that. Lose ten pounds, gain it back. Some attempts lasted two or three months. Some didn't survive a full week. I don't have a tidy narrative arc for that stretch of my life because there wasn't one. It was just a long series of restarts.

2014 gave me my first real health scare as an adult. I had the worst headache of my life, called 911 because I genuinely thought I was having a stroke, and my blood pressure measured 210 over 145. I spent five days in the hospital just getting that number down to something they were willing to release me at. CT scans found nothing structurally wrong. That hospitalization is where my adult chronic health history actually starts, on paper anyway. My asthma, which I'd had as a kid and outgrown, came back not long after the weight gain caught up with me.

2018 added the next piece. My A1C came back elevated for the first time I'd actually checked it, though I'd gone years without seeing a doctor regularly, so there's a real chance it had already been elevated for a while before anyone measured it. I never needed insulin. Oral medication kept me inside the pre-diabetic range, technically managed, never actually resolved.

Then came January 25, 2020, which is the morning everything else in this chapter has been leading up to.

I was at my heaviest. I'd just gotten out of the shower when my heart started racing, hard enough that I noticed it immediately. Renata is the closest thing our household has to a family doctor, so I asked her to check, and she grabbed the stethoscope from my blood pressure monitor to listen. She'd never heard a heartbeat do what mine was doing. We got in the car for urgent care. About two minutes after leaving the house, we both had the same realization at the same time: this wasn't an urgent care problem. Urgent care was just going to take one look and send us straight to the ER anyway. So we turned around, went home, and called 911 instead.

They identified AFib almost immediately. My heart rate was in the 220s. I did not know, before that morning, that atrial fibrillation could be that dramatic. They rushed me in, and there was already a team of doctors and nurses waiting in a special room by the time we arrived. IV medication brought my rate down to somewhere between 150 and 170, and they told me it was going to reset my heart. I was out of it enough that I had no real idea what that meant in practice. I just remember ending up in an ER room afterward, reunited with Renata, who at that point knew even less about what had actually happened in that room than I did.

After a little over an hour, and a second dose of the same medication, I started getting tunnel vision. It felt like my soul was being pulled out through my toes, which is the only way I've ever been able to describe it accurately. I remember asking Renata to call the doctors back in. The last thing I remember at all is looking at the heart rate monitor and watching the number sit in the high 50s.

Everything after that is Renata's memory, not mine, and I think it needs to stay in her words.

What Renata remembers:

When I called the nurse, I didn't fully understand what was happening, but within a minute or two we had four or five people in that room. One of them was already prepping the defibrillator, because Marcio was unconscious by then. Everything moved very fast, fast enough that I can't hand you a clean, organized account of it, and then I heard the beep. Marcio had flatlined.

Time stopped for me right there. Imagine pausing a video, except the flatline beep is still playing. I genuinely could not believe what I was hearing. My very first thought, immediately, was how I was going to tell our kids and his parents that he had died. I stood there in disbelief while the nurses lifted his gown to get the defibrillator into position.

I can't tell you with certainty how long he was actually out. It felt like forever standing there. If I had to estimate based on everything that happened in that window, it was somewhere around fifteen to twenty seconds. Then the steady flatline tone stopped, and he came back on his own, without them ever needing to actually shock him.

A beep here, a beep there, a beep everywhere. Honestly, at this point it could become a song.

Back to me. I remember waking up the way I wake up from any faint, I've had a lot of practice at that specific skill, with a tingling sensation on my lips. The first thing I saw was my heart rate sitting steady in the mid seventies, like absolutely nothing had happened. The doctor came in and explained that the plan had worked, the medication had done its job, my heart had reset, and I was back in sinus rhythm. That was the first time in my life I'd heard "sinus" used for anything other than an infection.

I followed up with a cardiologist, who connected me with a cardiac electrophysiologist, and after three or four more hospitalizations, the treatment actually held. No more AFib episodes since. But that morning is what finally put my diet in order, in a way that nothing before it had managed to.

I was up near 340 pounds by then. I got down to 250, which felt like a real number, a number with some weight behind it. That's also when I started weight training for the first time, and somewhere in the middle of building that habit, I looked up and realized I was back at 310. Ozempic became the next answer. I took the wrong starting dose, 1 milligram right out of the gate instead of easing in at a quarter milligram like I was supposed to, and lost 50 pounds in six months anyway, landing at 260. I started weight training again around then too, and things got strange in a way I still can't fully explain, it's almost like the weight training itself became the thing working against the number on the scale. Even with the medication still in my system, I started gaining again. By the time I hit 290, the bariatric option was the one still standing.

You already know the rest.

One more piece of this worth saying honestly, because it might matter to you if you're anywhere near this decision yourself: my primary care doctor did not recommend the surgery. I'd been her patient for years. Her position was that the Ozempic was already keeping my A1C at an acceptable pre-diabetic level, and that it was working, so why escalate. I knew, with total certainty, that the moment I stopped the medication, the weight would come right back the way it always had. Against her advice, advocating for myself in a way I hadn't really done before in a doctor's office, I went straight to a surgeon instead.

It wasn't only her. Even close friends who knew we were considering it would come back with some discouraging story. Someone knew someone who'd had it done and it ruined their life. Someone else knew a friend who gained every pound back within a couple of years. I don't think any of them meant to discourage us. They were just sharing the one specific story they personally knew. But if you let other people's specific, secondhand stories make your decision for you, you will talk yourself out of it every single time. Find a serious surgeon and have the real conversation about your actual case. They are the only source of direction that's actually looking at you, not someone else's story about someone else's body.

For years, I could lose weight just fine. That was never the hard part. The hard part was that I loved to cook, and I didn't cook like a man trying to lose weight. I cooked like a man auditioning for a food network show nobody asked me to audition for. Rich sauces. Real butter. Portions sized for a linebacker, served to a guy who worked from a laptop.

I once gave up chocolate for eight years. Eight years. I should have gotten a medal, or at least a participation trophy shaped like a cocoa bean. It didn't matter. I'd lose weight, hit 260 pounds like clockwork, and then the plateau would show up right on schedule, like an unwelcome relative who never RSVPs but always arrives exactly at dinner time. Frustration would win, the weight would come back, and we'd do the whole dance again. If plateaus gave out frequent flyer miles, I'd have had enough for a free trip to Rome by now.

Renata's version of the fight looked different. She had actual discipline, the kind I lacked. When she committed, she lost the weight completely, no negotiating. But she'd hold onto it for a year or two and then it would quietly let itself back in, like it still had a key to the house.

By the time we stopped trying to out-stubborn this thing on our own, I was back up near 340 pounds, AFib and flatline behind me but never far from my mind, my A1C sitting at 6.5 to 6.8 and managed by medication that was only going to have more work to do as time went on. Renata was at 220, dealing with sleep apnea and joint and back pain that had stopped being something you just push through.

She decided first. She was going to go first too, that was the plan, right up until we both remembered one important detail about me: I faint at blood and medical procedures. Not "feels a little queasy." Faints. Full vasovagal shutdown, lights out, timber. If I had seen her fresh out of surgery, stitches and all, before my own date, there was a very real chance I would have chickened out of the whole thing from a horizontal position on a hospital floor. So Renata, graciously, agreed to flip the order and let me go first, protecting me from my own nervous system like she has been doing for over twenty years.

Here's the plot twist neither of us saw coming, and the part that makes it funny in hindsight: when she saw me post op, she was the one who had second thoughts, despite having actual medical assistant training from high school. She'd seen recoveries before. None of that mattered once it was me on the bed, stitches and all. She'll tell you she almost chickened out on my behalf, days before her own surgery. I'm fairly sure she was hamming it up more than she'd ever admit, because it genuinely did look worse than it was, and we both had great recoveries. But there was a solid moment where the woman with actual clinical training stood there looking at me like she was quietly reconsidering all of her life choices.

We started the process in August 2024. Clearances, evaluations, paperwork that made buying a house look casual. Our BMIs, my pre-diabetes, and her sleep apnea made us automatic candidates on paper, but we still needed the psychological, cardiac, and primary care sign-offs before anyone would actually schedule a date. It was not fast, and it was not supposed to be.

Here's the part nobody prepares you for: the pre-op diet is worse than the surgery itself. My liquid diet started right around Thanksgiving, which felt like scheduling a juice cleanse to start the same week as the Super Bowl. I sat at a table full of people eating everything I'd normally have seconds of, sipping something that tasted like sadness in a cup, and I got through it by mentally teleporting myself into the future. Not the future where I fit into smaller pants. The future where I had enough years and enough energy to actually keep up with my kids.

We scheduled our surgeries two weeks apart on purpose. Mine on December 2, 2024. Hers on the 16th. The idea was simple: whoever went first would be steady enough to take care of the other. It worked exactly like that, and it might be the most functional piece of relationship planning either of us has ever pulled off.

That was the plan, anyway. What nobody handed us was a manual for what the body actually does next, and it turned out to have a few surprises on the schedule.

Chapter 3: The First Year, Uncensored

Same surgeon. Same post op team. Same everything, at least on paper. We were told, more than once, that no two people's results are the same, and we nodded seriously and then compared our progress anyway approximately every single day, because we are human beings and not robots governed entirely by logic. Some weeks I was convinced it wasn't working for me. Some weeks she felt the exact same about herself. We ended up in the same place though: she lost more relative to her height, and I landed in a longer, quieter phase of body recomposition, trading fat for muscle in a process so slow it makes watching paint dry look like an action movie. The scale barely moves some months. The mirror moves even slower. Nobody warns you that the hardest phase is also the most boring one to explain at a dinner party.

Learning where the actual limit sat, in those first six months, was not a graceful process. One extra spoonful past the line, and my new pouch did not file a polite complaint. It staged a full uprising. Food would simply turn around and take the elevator straight back up, express service, no stops, no warning, no negotiation. The first time it happened I remember looking genuinely offended, like I'd been personally betrayed by a bite of scrambled eggs I had trusted completely. Renata got just as fast at reading the exact look on my face right before it happened as I got at recognizing hers. It was, without question, the most humbling crash course either of us has ever taken in listening to a body that had very suddenly, very firmly, stopped negotiating.

One genuine surprise, worth stating plainly because it defies what everyone expects: the dumping syndrome everyone warns you about never really showed up for either of us. If I overdo sweets now, on the rare occasion I do, what I get is a sugar crash, not the textbook dumping reaction people describe. It's manageable with a bit of discipline. It's not what the pamphlets promised. It's just what turned out to be true for us, and if our surgeon is reading this, no, we are not asking for a refund.

Here's a surprise that went the other direction: the hair loss is real, and no amount of biotin, vitamins, or extra protein is going to negotiate you out of it if you're genetically wired for it. We both were. Renata got the worse end of it by far. There were days in the shower it came out in enough volume that it genuinely felt like it might start a conversation with her. It was startling every single time, no matter how many times we told ourselves it was normal and expected. Eventually it stopped. Some of it grew back. Some of it didn't. That's just the honest version, not the version with a bow on it.

Two more honest entries for the "what actually happens" file. The stitches and scars fade far more than you'd expect. A year out, mine are nearly invisible, and if you're a man with some body hair, nature handles the camouflage for free, no cream or oil required. It's one of those worries that looms large going in and then quietly resolves itself while you're busy worrying about other things.

Skin sagging is real too, we won't pretend otherwise, but for us it landed nowhere near as bad as the horror stories had us bracing for. That one is deeply personal though, it depends on age, genetics, how much you lose, how fast, and a dozen other factors nobody can predict for you. Results vary, genuinely, and this is one of the places they vary the most. If it's a real concern for you, it's a conversation for your surgeon, who can actually look at your situation instead of guessing from a page.

So here's the actual choice, laid out as plainly as we can put it. Look at the before pictures next to the after pictures and decide for yourself. On one side: a body that finally moves the way you want it to, health numbers that stopped scaring your doctor, energy to keep up with your own life. On the other side: the extra weight, the pain that doesn't let up, the version of you that was slowly getting boxed in by your own body, but with a completely full head of hair. When we put it that way to ourselves, it stopped being a hard decision very quickly.

A quick word on who we actually are, because it matters for what comes next. We like to say we were made in Brazil with European parts and assembled in the US, raised in Miami since we were kids. My roots run through Italy, Portugal, and Britain. Hers run through Spain, Italy, and Germany. What every branch of both families has in common is this: nobody in our lineage has ever once believed that a guest could leave the table hungry. We grew up in kitchens where seconds weren't a choice, they were a love language, and where "I already ate" was treated less like a statement of fact and more like an opening offer in a negotiation you were going to lose.

Which is exactly why, before surgery, our real fear wasn't the operation. It was what came after, whether we'd ever feel normal again. In our culture food isn't just food. It's the whole event. It's how you say "I love you" without saying it out loud, several times a week, with garlic bread involved. We were genuinely afraid that fixing our health would cost us that.

It didn't. Once the initial shock wore off, we found our way back to the table. Food just stopped being the main event. People were. That single shift changed more than our health numbers. It changed our marriage, in more ways than we'll get into here. There's a whole chapter near the end of this book just for that, and we've already warned our kids to skip it.

Our trips are better too, because we actually have the energy to be in them, hiking together instead of one of us waiting in the air conditioned lobby "guarding the bags." If only one of us had done this, I think it would have carved out a gap between us that would have been hard to close later. Doing it together closed that gap before it ever opened.

We stayed mostly in sync, though never identical, because we were never actually racing, even on the days that took real effort to remember. Between months three and six, my BMI sat lower than hers, simply because I'd gone first and had a two week head start on healing and adjusting. Renata is more disciplined than I am, genuinely, and there were still weeks that gap got to her anyway, a quiet disappointment she'd try to wave off before I noticed. I kept saying the same thing back to her every time it came up: our bodies are two different organisms running two different timelines, and the only comparison that ever mattered was Renata against her own effort, not Renata against my numbers. She's the one who eventually explained body recomposition to me in a way that actually made sense, the scale and the mirror don't run on the same clock, and neither do two different people's bodies. These days I sit around a 25.5 BMI and she's closer to 20, and neither number means one of us is winning. We just keep showing up for ourselves, and we celebrate whatever the other one's body decides to do with that effort.

Officially, year two starts for us three months from the day I'm writing this. Unofficially, it already started, because of what happened this year, and that's the entire reason this book exists.

I went to visit my grandfather abroad, and I let myself indulge. Not recklessly. Just with a version of "moderation" that had quietly lost its actual meaning somewhere over the Atlantic. Cozy, rich, comfort food, the kind that doesn't ask permission before adding itself to your hips. I gained 12 pounds in a month. Twelve. In the time it takes most people to finish a Netflix series, I'd undone what felt like months of work.

It scared me badly. Some of it turned out to be fluid retention, not fat, but that detail didn't comfort me in the moment. What mattered was the wake up call: the surgery is a tool, and like any tool, it does not care about your feelings. It will force weight loss at the start almost no matter what you do, the same way a new gym membership will absolutely get you in shape, right up until the day you stop showing up and it becomes an expensive place to store your car keys. If I couldn't make this work after everything we'd been through, I genuinely didn't know what else was supposed to.

Here's the part I want you to actually sit with, because it's the whole point: over the following three months, I took back 8 of those 12 pounds. Not with a crash diet. Not with panic. Just by picking the tool back up and using it the way it was meant to be used. I still have 4 pounds of ground left to close, and I'm not pretending otherwise. But those 8 pounds are proof, not a victory lap. Proof that the fear I had, that one bad month meant I'd broken something permanently, was wrong. It wasn't permanent. It was just a signal to pay attention again.

All of that was the first year. The second year is where this book actually earns its title, because the second year is where it almost got away from me.

Chapter 4: Year Two, Where It Almost Got Away From Me

Part of "using the tool correctly" for me turned out to be the gym, though I didn't exactly sprint there. I got medically cleared to start weight training in the spring. I didn't actually start until the end of summer. Five months of being fashionably late to my own recovery, and the excuse was almost funny: I was afraid that if I started lifting, body recomposition would make the scale plateau even faster than it already threatens to, and I'd have to sit with that quiet, invisible phase even longer. So instead of getting ahead of it, I just avoided the whole gym entirely, which is a bit like refusing to learn to swim because you're worried you might get good at it.

Once I actually started, five months after being cleared, I built something I'd never really had before: a real habit. Three times a week, no negotiating with myself. The only times I've missed were when I was sick or, once, dealing with a muscle injury. That's it. That's the entire list of exceptions in over a year.

Part of why the gym mattered so much is something nobody warns you about either: my own center of gravity had quietly relocated without leaving a forwarding address. I wanted the posture of a man who'd earned his new frame, shoulders back, spine doing whatever proud spines are supposed to do. What I actually had were muscles that had spent years letting all that extra weight do half the structural work for free, and the moment the weight left, those muscles had no idea what their actual job was anymore. I ended up in functional range therapy, plus a set of targeted exercises, essentially reteaching my own body how to hold itself up like an adult, one unglamorous session at a time. Losing the weight was only ever half the assignment. The other half was teaching what's left of you how to stand up straight again.

Somewhere in there, my 18 year old daughter called me a gym rat. Not in a school essay, not in a birthday card, just tossed off in conversation like it was an established fact about me, the same way you'd mention someone's eye color. I have been called a lot of things in my life. That is, without question, the best one. Eighteen year olds do not hand out compliments to their parents for sport. If she's noticed, it's real.

And the plateau still came, because of course it did. I hit 250 pounds and watched the number sit there, and every old alarm bell in my head went off at once. This was the same setup as every plateau that had ever beaten me before. My brain was already writing the regain story before it happened. But this time it lasted ten days. Ten. Not the endless standoff I remembered from before surgery. The needle started moving again, because this time the tool was actually being used the way it was designed to be used, gym and all.

That fear is exactly why we're writing this. And if I'm being honest about the other reason: somewhere in the middle of all of it, I realized I wasn't writing this book for you first. I was writing it for me. I needed to say all of this out loud, in order, to actually believe it myself. If it helps you too, that's not the accident. That's the whole design.

Chapter 5: Not Ready Yet? Here's What You Can Start Now

Maybe you're not sold on surgery. Maybe you're scared, or your family's not on board, or you're still hoping willpower finds a way this time. Wherever you are in that decision, none of it is wasted time if you use it deliberately instead of just waiting.

Things worth starting now, regardless of what you eventually decide:

  • Start a simple food log, even a bad one. Not for restriction, for information. Most people are shocked by what a week of honest logging actually shows them. This alone is useful data whether surgery ends up in your future or not.

  • Get your labs and vitals on record now, and again in three months. A1C, blood pressure, cholesterol. If you do end up pursuing surgery, having a documented trend line, not just a single snapshot, makes the qualification process faster and gives your own team a clearer picture.

  • Have the conversation with your primary doctor early, even just to ask questions. You don't need to commit to anything by asking. Most people wait until they're fully decided to bring it up, which just delays getting real answers by months.

  • Start building one habit you could keep either way. A short daily walk. Drinking more water. Something small enough to actually stick, since "I'll fix everything at once, someday" is exactly the pattern that keeps a lot of people stuck at the plateau we described in Chapter 2.

  • Read real accounts, not just marketing. Support groups, forums, books like this one. Get the unfiltered version before you decide, not after.

One honest thing worth sitting with: the fear of the decision itself often outlasts the decision. Whatever you choose, surgery or not, right now or later, making the choice deliberately, with real information, beats drifting into either option by default. Take your time. Just don't confuse taking your time with taking no action at all.

If you read Chapter 2, you already know we didn't take our own advice here as fast as we should have. Years of plateaus, an eight year chocolate fast that solved nothing, a slow build toward AFib and 340 pounds before either of us actually moved. The information doesn't have to be perfect and the decision doesn't have to be immediate. It just has to actually move, eventually, instead of running the same loop indefinitely.

Chapter 6: You've Decided. Here's the Actual Roadmap

Deciding is the easy part, emotionally speaking. What comes next is a genuinely confusing stretch of clearances, paperwork, and waiting, and almost nobody explains the shape of it in advance. Here's the roadmap, in the order it typically happens, based on what we actually went through.

1. Initial consultation and qualification screening. Your surgeon's office checks the basics: BMI, and whether you have qualifying conditions, things like pre-diabetes, sleep apnea, high blood pressure, joint issues. Some people qualify on BMI alone. Others qualify through a combination of BMI and a related health condition. This part is mostly paperwork and a conversation.

2. The clearance gauntlet. This is the stretch that surprises people with how long it takes. Expect to need sign-off from several different directions, and your specific list may vary by program:

  • Primary care clearance, confirming you're generally healthy enough for surgery

  • Cardiac clearance, especially if you have any heart-related risk factors

  • Psychological clearance, covered in its own section next, since it deserves one

  • Nutritional counseling, often multiple sessions before surgery is even scheduled

  • Insurance authorization, which can be its own multi-week saga depending on your plan

3. The pre-op diet. In the final stretch before surgery, most programs put you on a liquid or very low calorie diet, partly to shrink the liver and make the procedure safer. This is often the single hardest week or two of the entire process, harder for a lot of people than the actual recovery. Plan your calendar around it if you can. Ours landed across a major holiday, and we did not get to pick that timing.

4. Surgery day, and the short, intense post-op stretch. Different procedures, different timelines, but expect a hospital stay of one to a few days, followed by a staged return to food, typically liquids, then purees, then soft foods, then regular food, over the following weeks.

Practical tips for surviving this whole stretch:

  • Start a single dedicated folder, physical or digital, the moment you get your first piece of paperwork. More on this in the next section.

  • Ask each provider directly what they still need from you before you leave that appointment. Don't assume the office will chase you down.

  • Build in buffer time. Nearly every stage of this process takes longer than the optimistic timeline you'll be given at the start.

  • If two weeks apart, or any staggered timing, makes sense for your situation the way it did for ours, ask your team directly whether that's medically reasonable for you. It gave us built-in caretakers for each other during the hardest days.

If you read Chapter 2, this is the exact stretch we walked through starting in August 2024, clearances, evaluations, and a pre-op diet that landed right across Thanksgiving. We didn't invent this roadmap from a textbook. This is just what actually happened to us, laid out plainly enough that you can see the shape of your own version coming.

None of this replaces your actual program's specific instructions, every practice runs this slightly differently. But knowing the general shape of the road ahead of time makes the whole thing feel a lot less like wandering in the dark.

Chapter 7: RNY or Sleeve? The Fork in the Road

Somewhere between your first consultation and your surgery date, you'll hit the biggest decision in this entire process, and it usually arrives faster than you feel ready for. Gastric sleeve or gastric bypass. Two procedures, two different mechanisms, two different sets of tradeoffs, and a lot of very confident opinions from people who are not your surgeon.

We're going to say the important part first, because everything else in this section is downstream of it: we are not going to tell you which one to get, and you should be suspicious of anyone who does. The right procedure depends on your medical history, your existing conditions, your medications, your anatomy, and things that only show up on a scope or a scan. That decision belongs to you and your surgeon, in a room, with your actual chart open.

What we can do is explain what each one is, what people actually weigh when deciding, and what questions to walk into that room with. And we'll tell you how it went for us, because our two cases happen to illustrate the two most common reasons surgeons steer patients one way or the other.

What Each Procedure Actually Does

The sleeve, formally the vertical sleeve gastrectomy, removes a large portion of the stomach and leaves behind a narrow tube, roughly banana-shaped, running from your esophagus to your intestine. Nothing is rerouted. The path food takes is the same path it always took, through a much smaller container. Because a portion of the stomach is permanently removed, it is not reversible.

The bypass, formally the Roux-en-Y gastric bypass, usually shortened to RNY, creates a small pouch at the top of the stomach and connects it directly to a lower section of the small intestine, bypassing the rest of the stomach and the first stretch of intestine. So it does two things at once: it restricts how much you can eat, and it changes how and where nutrients get absorbed. The original stomach stays in your body, still doing some work, just no longer in the path food travels.

That second mechanism, the rerouting, is the source of nearly every meaningful difference between the two procedures, in both directions.

What People Actually Weigh

These are the tradeoffs that come up in real consultations. None of them is a scoreboard, and every one of them lands differently depending on the patient.

Reflux and GERD. This is one of the biggest deciding factors. The sleeve can worsen reflux for some patients, and severe pre-existing GERD is a common reason surgeons steer toward bypass instead. Bypass frequently improves reflux. If heartburn is a real part of your life, this will likely come up early in your consultation.

Type 2 diabetes. Bypass has a well-established track record with diabetes remission, and the effect often shows up remarkably fast, sometimes before significant weight loss has even happened, because of the hormonal and absorption changes from the rerouting. For a patient with diabetes or significant pre-diabetes, this frequently tips the scale.

Nutrient absorption and supplements. Because bypass reroutes past a section of intestine where absorption happens, the lifelong supplement and monitoring burden is generally higher, and deficiency risk is more significant if you stop. Sleeve patients still need supplements and labs, but the malabsorption piece is less pronounced. If you know yourself to be someone who will not reliably take pills forever, say that out loud to your surgeon. It's a legitimate input, not a confession.

Medications. Absorption changes after bypass can affect how some medications work, including certain psychiatric medications and extended-release formulations. If you're on anything long-term, this is a specific conversation to have, ideally with both your surgeon and your prescribing doctor.

Complexity and complication profile. Bypass is the more complex operation with more connection points. The complication profiles differ between the two procedures, and your surgeon can walk you through what that means for someone with your specific risk factors.

Reversibility and revision. Neither of these is a casual undo. The sleeve is not reversible, since stomach tissue is removed. Bypass is technically reversible in rare circumstances but is not treated as a reversible operation in practice. Revision from sleeve to bypass is a relatively common path years down the line, often for reflux; the reverse is much less common. Ask your surgeon what revision options would look like from whichever procedure you're considering.

Weight loss outcomes. Both procedures work. Studies generally show bypass producing somewhat more total weight loss on average, with sleeve close behind, and enormous individual variation in both. Anyone quoting you a precise number for what you personally will lose is guessing.

How It Went For Us

We both had RNY, and we got there for two completely different reasons.

For me, it was diabetes. I had an A1C sitting at 6.5 to 6.8, managed with medication that was only going to have more work to do over time. Before I made any decision, I reached out to a friend who is both a physician and a sleeve patient himself, which is a genuinely useful combination of perspectives to have access to. He'd had the sleeve. He recommended I get the bypass, specifically because of the diabetes. When a doctor who chose a different procedure for himself tells you to choose the other one for your particular situation, that is worth listening closely to. It's the opposite of the usual advice you get, which is people recommending whatever they happened to have.

For Renata, it was reflux. She'd had terrible heartburn for years, the kind that shapes what you eat and when you lie down. That history alone pointed toward bypass rather than sleeve.

Then her surgery produced the plot twist. Once the surgeon was actually in there, he found a hiatal hernia that nobody knew about beforehand. It hadn't shown up in any of the pre-op workup. He corrected it on the spot and adjusted course accordingly. She went in for one operation and came out having also had a problem fixed that she'd been unknowingly living with, and which had almost certainly been driving the reflux the whole time.

We mention that not to alarm anyone, but because it's a genuinely useful thing to know going in: your surgeon may find something on the day, and part of what you're choosing when you choose a surgeon is someone whose judgment you trust to make a call while you're asleep.

Both of our procedures were done with robotic laparoscopy, using the da Vinci system, by the same surgeon, who happened to be a fellow Italian, which meant our pre-op consultations included a certain amount of conversation about food that was arguably off-topic and definitely necessary.

When Something Actually Goes Wrong

Every honest conversation about this surgery has to include the complications, and we're going to give you a real one instead of a paragraph of statistics, because a real one is more useful.

In March, roughly fifteen months after her surgery, Renata developed severe abdominal pain that came on hard and did not let up. She had an internal hernia, a section of intestine had pushed through an opening and become trapped, causing a partial bowel obstruction. She needed semi-urgent surgery to correct it.

Here's the part we want you to actually take away: internal hernia is a recognized complication of gastric bypass specifically. When the intestine is rerouted during RNY, the procedure creates spaces in the tissue that can, sometimes years later, allow a loop of bowel to slip through and get caught. It is not common, and it is not something most patients will ever experience. But it is real, it is associated with this procedure, and it characteristically shows up long after you've stopped thinking of yourself as a surgical patient. That delay is exactly what makes it dangerous, because by then you've mentally filed the surgery under "done."

The timing was, as these things go, spectacularly inconvenient. I was at a conference in Salt Lake City and could not get home in time. Our daughter, who had turned eighteen a few months earlier, was suddenly the adult in the room, handling paperwork and decisions that nobody prepares you for at that age.

She has a phrase she's used for years, long before any of this: "adult premium." Not just a legal adult, but the upgraded version, someone with real life experience behind them. She'd always wanted that status. She earned it in a hospital corridor that week, and she'd tell you so herself. She starts nursing school soon, which now feels less like a coincidence than a continuation.

The Question That Changed Everything

Here is the part of this story we most want you to read carefully.

After the first CT scan showed the obstruction, the plan was to open her up. A full vertical incision down the abdomen, a surgeon's hands physically inside her, that night. The reason wasn't that this was the best approach. It was that the bariatric surgeon on call wasn't available until the next morning, and the physician there had to work with what he had.

They were already preparing her for surgery when she asked one question: is this a life-or-death emergency right now, or is it urgent?

That question made him pause. He reassessed. He ordered more imaging, this time with contrast, and the clearer picture showed something the first scan hadn't: the blockage was partial, not complete. She was in real pain, but she was stable, and it was safe to wait until morning.

The next day, a bariatric surgeon repaired it laparoscopically. Small incisions. Far shorter recovery. No vertical scar down her abdomen.

She did not refuse care. She did not argue, delay, or diagnose herself. She asked one clarifying question about urgency, and a good physician responded to it by getting better information. That is the whole lesson.

If you are ever in that position, the question is: is this a life-or-death emergency right now, or is it urgent? In a true emergency the answer will be immediate and unambiguous, and you should proceed without hesitation. But if there's a pause in that answer, it may be worth asking what more information could be gathered first, and whether a specialist in your specific procedure could be involved. Emergency departments that don't see many bariatric patients are working without the context your own surgical team has.

Symptoms That Warrant an Emergency Room Visit

Not a phone call, not a wait-and-see:

  • Severe, persistent abdominal pain, particularly pain that comes in waves or keeps escalating
  • Nausea and vomiting that won't stop, especially if you can't keep fluids down
  • Abdominal bloating or distension paired with pain
  • Inability to pass gas or stool
  • Any pain that feels categorically different from the ordinary discomfort you've learned to expect

If you have had a bypass and you have severe abdominal pain, say the words "I have had a gastric bypass" to whoever is treating you, immediately and clearly. Standard imaging does not always catch an internal hernia, and your surgical history is the single most important piece of information in that room. You may need to be the one who supplies it.

Renata is fine. It was caught, it was corrected, and she recovered. And having been through it, neither of us would go back and undo the original decision. That's not us minimizing what happened. It's us telling you that a real complication happened to us, it was handled, and the surgery is still the best decision we ever made about our health.

Ask your surgeon what your specific risks are, what symptoms should send you to an emergency room, and what you should tell the staff when you get there. Then remember the answer, because you may need it years from now, on a random Tuesday, long after you've stopped thinking about any of this.

Questions to Bring to Your Consultation

Write these down and take them with you. You will not remember them in the room.

  • Based on my specific medical history, which procedure do you recommend, and what's the reasoning?
  • How does my reflux history, if any, factor into that recommendation?
  • How does my diabetes or pre-diabetes status factor in?
  • What will my lifelong supplement requirements look like with each option?
  • What labs will I need, how often, and for how long?
  • How would my current medications be affected?
  • What are the complication rates for this procedure, in your hands specifically, for patients like me?
  • What would revision look like later if it were ever needed?
  • Will this be done laparoscopically, robotically, or open, and why?
  • How many of these have you performed?

That last one is not rude. It's one of the most reasonable questions in medicine, and any good surgeon expects it.

The Part Worth Remembering

Both procedures are tools. Neither one works on its own, and everything else in this book, the fullness signal, the protein, the plateaus, the year two drift, applies to both.

We've met people who love their sleeve and people who love their bypass, and we've met people from both groups who regained because they stopped using the tool. The procedure you choose matters. It matters less than what you do with it for the next thirty years.

Chapter 9: What Psychological Clearance Actually Looks Like

This one deserves its own section because it's the step people seem most anxious about going in, usually because nobody explains what it actually involves, and the phrase "psychological evaluation" does a lot of unnecessary heavy lifting in people's imaginations beforehand.

Here's the honest version: it's mostly a conversation. The provider is checking for things like your understanding of the lifestyle changes ahead, whether you have any untreated conditions that could complicate recovery or eating behavior, your support system, and your general readiness for a change this significant. It is not a pass or fail test of your worth as a person, even though it can feel that way walking in.

That said, some parts of it can genuinely catch you off guard. I was asked, at one point, to spell the word "earth" backwards. Out loud. In real time. For a basic cognitive screening question that I am certain is completely routine and means nothing about my intelligence, I nearly lost the whole thing trying to reverse five letters under pressure like I was on a game show with a studio audience watching. If something like that happens to you, know in advance that it's a standard screening tool, not a secret trap designed to catch you off guard, even though it absolutely will catch you off guard anyway.

A few things that can actually help going in:

  • Answer honestly, not strategically. Providers do this constantly and can generally tell when someone's performing readiness rather than describing it.

  • If you have a history of anxiety, depression, disordered eating, or any other mental health condition, expect it to come up, and know that having a history doesn't automatically disqualify you. What matters is whether it's being managed and whether your team knows about it.

  • Bring your actual support system into the conversation honestly. If it's thin, say so. That's useful information for your team, not a reason to fail the appointment.

  • It's completely normal to be nervous. Say that out loud if it helps. Nobody in that room is expecting you to be perfectly composed.

This step exists to protect you, not to gatekeep you from something you've already decided you need. Most people walk out having felt heard more than judged, once the nerves settle and the appointment turns out to be a conversation instead of an interrogation.

This ties back to something we mentioned in Chapter 3, our biggest fear going in wasn't the surgery itself, it was whether we'd still feel normal afterward. The psychological evaluation is really just your team checking for the same thing, from the other side of the table, before you even get there.

Chapter 10: Getting Home: The First Days

This section is about the daily, practical, nobody-tells-you-this stuff, not medical instructions. Your discharge paperwork covers the medical side, wound care, medication, activity restrictions, and that paperwork always wins if anything here conflicts with it. This is just the logistics nobody thinks to mention.

Set up before you go in, not after you get home. The version of you coming home from surgery will not want to be assembling anything, reorganizing a kitchen, or making decisions about where things go. Do that work in advance.

  • Pre-portion whatever liquids or purees your program has cleared for the first stretch, so you're not measuring anything with a sore midsection and low energy

  • Put daily essentials at counter height or eye level in the kitchen. Bending, reaching, and stretching are all going to be harder and more uncomfortable than usual for a while

  • Set up a comfortable resting spot with everything you'll actually want nearby, water, phone charger, whatever you'll be doing to pass time, so you're not making repeated trips

  • If you live with someone, plan who's doing what for the first week before surgery day, not during it. Decision fatigue is real when you're recovering, and having already talked through it removes one more thing to negotiate mid-recovery

A few other things worth knowing in advance:

  • Fatigue in the first stretch tends to be more intense than people expect, even for a relatively minor procedure by surgical standards. Plan for genuinely doing less than you think you'll need to.

  • Small daily wins matter more than they sound like they should. Getting dressed, a short walk to the mailbox, these are real progress in the first week, not consolation prizes.

  • If you have a partner or a friend doing this around the same time, staggering surgery dates, if your team says it's medically appropriate for your situation, means someone stable is around during the other person's hardest days. That structure did more for our recovery than almost anything else in this whole section.

  • Give yourself permission to be bored, uncomfortable, and a little emotional. It's a real transition, not just a physical one, and treating the emotional side as expected rather than alarming tends to make it pass faster.

This is exactly what we set up for ourselves in Chapter 2, surgeries two weeks apart on purpose, December 2nd and December 16th, so whoever went first would be steady enough to take care of the other. It remains one of the most functional pieces of planning either of us has ever pulled off, and it made this exact stretch dramatically easier than doing it alone would have.

Chapter 11: The First Month: Learning to Dose Your Food

Somewhere around the transition to soft foods and then regular food, a genuinely strange new skill shows up that nobody fully prepares you for: learning to dose your own eating, in real time, with a margin for error that's suddenly much smaller than it used to be. We told the exaggerated, comedic version of this story back in Chapter 3, the food taking the elevator back up, no stops. Here's the practical version.

What actually helps during this stretch, based on what we learned the hard way:

  • Smaller bites than feel necessary, and more chewing than feels necessary. Your instinct is going to default to old habits for a while. Deliberately overcorrecting, smaller and slower than seems required, tends to land closer to actually right.

  • Put the fork down between bites, on purpose, physically. This sounds almost silly written down, but it's one of the single most effective ways to actually catch your new fullness signal before you're past it, since it forces a pause your old habits won't naturally include.

  • Expect some trial and error, and don't treat every miss as a failure. Nearly everyone in this exact phase has a moment of misjudging the line. It's part of recalibrating a system that used to work completely differently.

  • Track it, even loosely, especially in this phase. Not for calorie obsession, for pattern recognition. If certain textures or types of food consistently cause discomfort, a log makes that pattern obvious fast instead of you having to piece it together from memory after the fact.

  • Bring specific questions to your dietitian instead of general ones. "This specific food caused this specific reaction, is that normal or worth flagging" gets a far more useful answer than "is what I'm eating okay."

The learning curve here is real, and it's steep for almost everyone, not just people who are somehow doing it wrong. Give yourself the same grace you'd give someone learning any genuinely new skill with real physical feedback attached to every mistake. You're not failing at eating. You're relearning it, on a much smaller margin than you had before, and that takes actual repetition to get right.

Chapter 12: The First Quarter: When the Shedding Stops (And You Panic)

Here's a moment that catches an enormous number of people off guard, early enough in the process that it feels like proof the surgery failed, when it's actually just proof your body is doing exactly what it's supposed to do.

The first stretch after surgery tends to bring dramatic, fast, almost effortless weight loss. It feels less like a habit you're building and more like a process happening to you, in the best possible way. Then, somewhere in the first few months, often right around the three month mark, it slows down hard, sometimes stopping almost entirely for a stretch.

This is not the same thing as the plateaus we talk about later in this book, the ones tied to habits and long-term maintenance. This is your body shifting from the initial dramatic loss phase into active healing and adjustment. Surgical trauma takes real energy and resources to recover from, internally, even once you feel fine on the outside. Hormones are recalibrating. Your body is, in a very real sense, busy with a different job than shedding weight for a while.

What tends to help during this specific stretch:

  • Know it's coming before it hits, so it registers as expected instead of alarming when it does

  • Keep doing exactly what you were doing, don't panic-restrict further or panic-abandon your habits, since this slowdown isn't a signal that something needs to change

  • Track things other than the scale during this window, energy, how clothes fit, strength, sleep, since the scale is genuinely not the most honest narrator during active healing

  • If it's paired with anything that actually feels wrong physically, not just "the number stopped moving," that's worth a call to your team, same as we covered in the plateau chapter later in this book

The single most common story we've heard from other people who went through this, and one that mirrors our own experience closely, is a moment of real fear that the surgery simply "didn't work," followed by relief a few weeks later when the loss picks back up on its own. If you hit this exact wall, we want you to have this section in your back pocket, so it reads as a known, expected chapter of the process instead of an emergency nobody warned you about.

It's worth mentioning that this isn't the last time the scale lies to you either. We talked in Chapter 3 about the much slower body recomposition phase further down the road, the one that makes watching paint dry look thrilling by comparison. This early quarter is just the first of several stretches where the number on the scale and the actual truth of what your body is doing quietly stop agreeing with each other.

Chapter 13: The Regain Fear Nobody Talks About

If you've made it this far in your own journey, you already know a fear that almost nobody says out loud at the support group meeting: the quiet, background hum of what if I regain it all. Not a passing worry. A low frequency dread that shows up uninvited at the exact moments you'd expect to feel proudest, a good scale morning, a compliment from a stranger, a pair of pants you haven't fit into in a decade.

Here's the part almost nobody tells you before surgery: that fear doesn't mean anything is wrong with you. It means you understand the stakes. You've already lived the version of this story where the weight came back, maybe more than once, and your brain is doing exactly what brains do after a betrayal like that. It's standing guard.

Why This Fear Is Different From Ordinary Worry

Most worries are about things that haven't happened yet. This one is different, because for most of us it already happened, repeatedly, before surgery. That's not anxiety inventing a threat out of nothing. That's pattern recognition doing its job with a genuinely accurate data set.

The problem is that the data set is now out of date. Everything your brain learned about weight loss, it learned in a body without this tool and without these habits. It's running old software on new hardware, and it will keep flagging false alarms until you deliberately teach it otherwise.

That's what most of this book is really doing. Every chapter that gives you a framework, a checklist, or a way to tell a normal wobble from a real problem is quietly retraining that alarm system to be accurate instead of just loud.

What the Fear Actually Does When You Leave It Unsupervised

Unmanaged, this fear does five predictable things, and naming them is most of the battle.

It catastrophizes normal fluctuation. Bodies move a few pounds in either direction constantly, from sodium, hydration, hormones, a hard workout, or a late dinner. Unmanaged fear reads every one of those as the first chapter of the regain story.

It makes you avoid information. This is the most damaging one. The fear says "don't weigh yourself, don't log it, you don't want to know," and then the avoidance itself becomes the actual risk. Nobody ever caught a problem early by refusing to look at it.

It turns single events into verdicts. One dinner out becomes evidence for the prosecution. One skipped gym session becomes proof you were never really the kind of person who does this.

It creates all-or-nothing swings. Fear pushes toward extremes: either white-knuckle perfection or complete abandonment. Both are the same instinct, and neither is sustainable.

It isolates you. The fear tells you nobody wants to hear about it, that you're supposed to be grateful and past this by now. So you stop mentioning it, and it grows in the dark.

Giving the Fear a Job

You don't get rid of this fear. We haven't. What you can do is put it to work.

Fear that has a job is genuinely useful: it checks your numbers weekly, notices when a habit has quietly slipped, catches the pattern that's forming before it hardens. Fear with no job just sits there running worst case scenarios at 2am.

The practical translation is simple. Give the fear scheduled work, at specific times, in specific ways.

  • A weekly check-in, on a set day. Not daily obsession, not months of avoidance. One consistent look at weight, measurements, and how the week actually went.
  • A written record. Fear is a terrible narrator working from memory and an excellent analyst working from data. Logging turns the same anxious energy into information.
  • One trusted person who hears the honest version. Not the whole internet. One person who gets the unedited update, including the bad weeks.
  • A pre-decided response. Decide now, while you're calm, what you'll do if the scale moves up four pounds. Having an answer already written removes most of the panic, because panic mostly comes from not knowing what to do next.

When the Fear Is 2am Loud

Some nights it will not be reasonable, and this section is for those nights.

Ask yourself one question: is there anything I can actually do about this right now? At 2am, the honest answer is almost always no. The scale is not going to tell you anything useful at 2am. Neither is the internet.

So the job at 2am is not solving. It's parking. Write the worry down, in one sentence, along with the one thing you'll actually check tomorrow. Then stop. You've given the fear an appointment, which is the only thing that ever gets it to sit down.

If the fear is loud most nights rather than occasional ones, if it's affecting your sleep, your eating, or your mood in a sustained way, that's genuinely worth raising with your doctor or a mental health professional. Anxiety around food and body after major weight loss is common, it's treatable, and treating it is not an admission of failure any more than getting the surgery was.

The Honest Ending

The fear doesn't fully go away, even when things are going well. We still feel it. The difference isn't that we stopped being afraid. It's that we stopped letting the fear drive, and started making it ride in the back seat, useful when it has something real to point out, quiet the rest of the time.

Notice, too, that the fear is proof of something worth keeping. People who don't care don't worry about this. The fear is your investment showing up as an emotion. It just needs a job description.

Chapter 14: Why the First-Year Playbook Stops Working

There's a day coming, or maybe it already came, when every book, app, and support group post written for you quietly stops applying. Nobody announces it. Here's how to see it coming and what to do on the other side of it.

Year one comes with training wheels bolted on so tightly you barely notice they're there. Frequent follow-up appointments. A strict, staged reintroduction of food that tells you exactly what to eat and when. A body that's losing weight so fast it feels like it's doing most of the work without asking your opinion. Nearly every bariatric cookbook, every support group thread, every piece of content out there is written for this exact phase, because it's dramatic, it's fast, and it's easy to give clear instructions for.

Then, somewhere around the twelve to eighteen month mark, the training wheels come off, usually without anyone announcing it. Appointments space out. The rapid loss slows down or stops. The rigid food rules give way to "just make good choices," which sounds supportive right up until you realize nobody actually told you what that means day to day anymore.

The Four Things That Change At Once

What makes this transition disorienting isn't any single change. It's that four separate supports withdraw at roughly the same time, and most people only consciously notice one of them.

The external structure withdraws. Fewer appointments means fewer deadlines, fewer scheduled moments of accountability, fewer natural checkpoints where someone asks how it's going and you have to answer out loud.

The physical feedback withdraws. In year one, the scale rewarded you constantly. That reward was doing enormous motivational work, and you don't notice how much until it stops. Behavior that used to produce visible results now produces maintenance, which feels like nothing when you're used to fireworks.

The social attention withdraws. People commented constantly in year one. By year two the transformation is old news, you look like yourself now, and the compliments that were quietly fueling you have moved on to someone else's news.

The clear rules withdraw. "Purees this week, soft foods next week" is a rule. "Make good choices" is a mood. Going from precise instructions to open-ended judgment is a real skill transition that nobody frames as one.

Notice that three of those four are motivational, not nutritional. That's the actual problem. Most people go looking for a diet answer to what is really a structure problem.

The Content Gap Is Real, and It's Not Your Imagination

Go looking for guidance at month fourteen and you will find the same thing we found: another liquid diet recipe book, another "first 30 days" guide, another piece of content built for a version of you that no longer exists. It's a little like graduating from a structured training program and being handed a stack of beginner textbooks as your only resource going forward.

There are structural reasons for this. Year one is dramatic and photogenic and makes a better story. Year one advice is safer and easier to write, because the rules are standardized. And most people writing in this space are writing from their own year one, because that's when the urge to share is strongest.

Which means the exact moment you need guidance most is the moment the market serves you least. That's not a flaw in you. It's a gap in what gets written for people like us, and it's the entire reason this book exists in the first place.

Building Your Own Training Wheels

The fix is to deliberately rebuild each of the four supports that withdrew. Not all at once, and not perfectly, but on purpose rather than by accident.

Replace the external structure with a self-scheduled version. Put a recurring weekly check-in on your calendar, the same day each week, and treat it like an appointment someone else scheduled. Fifteen minutes. Weight, measurements, how the week actually went, one thing to adjust.

Replace scale feedback with other metrics. This is covered in depth in Chapter 26, but the short version: strength numbers, how clothes fit, energy, sleep quality, and lab results all keep moving when the scale has gone quiet. Start tracking at least one of them before the scale stalls, not after.

Replace social attention with a chosen witness. One person who gets the honest update, on purpose, on a schedule. The compliments stopped because the transformation stopped being news, not because your effort stopped mattering.

Replace prescribed rules with your own written ones. Not a diet. A short list of your own non-negotiables, decided while you're calm, so that "make good choices" has an actual definition you wrote yourself. Three or four is plenty. Chapter 20 walks through how to build them.

The Reframe That Actually Helps

Year one is a project. Year two onward is a practice.

Projects have a finish line, external deadlines, and visible progress. Practices have none of that. They're just the thing you do, repeatedly, at a level of intensity you could sustain for a decade.

Most of the distress in this transition comes from trying to run a practice with project psychology, waiting for the next milestone, the next dramatic result, the next moment of external validation. Those aren't coming at the same frequency anymore, and that's not a sign something has gone wrong.

Year two is quieter. That's the point. It's also, for most people, where the outcome actually gets decided.

Chapter 15: Checking In With Your Team

Your surgeon, your dietitian, and your primary doctor are the actual source of truth for your specific situation. Not us, not a Facebook group, not a podcast, and not the guy at the gym who read one article. This whole book sits downstream of that fact, and this chapter is about how to actually use that relationship well, instead of either blindly deferring or quietly ignoring it.

The Story That Explains This Whole Chapter

Not every piece of advice from your care team is going to fit your actual life, and that's worth saying out loud instead of pretending your team gets it right every time. A nurse practitioner once built a protein target for me around a fairly average post-op patient. I was not an average post-op patient. I was about to become someone who hits the gym three times a week like it's a part time job, and the number she gave me didn't have a box on the intake form for that.

Here's what I want to be very clear about: the answer is not "ignore your provider and do your own math instead." The answer is to go back. Bring your actual activity level. Ask directly whether the target accounts for it. If it doesn't feel right and your team can't explain why, ask for a referral to someone who specializes in active post-bariatric patients, a sports dietitian, not a general one. Push until the plan actually matches the person living it.

The lesson isn't "trust your gut over your provider." It's "your team can only build the right plan around the information they have, so hand them better information, loudly and often, before you go anywhere near adjusting things yourself."

Who Does What On Your Team

Most people are vague about which provider handles which question, and that vagueness costs you good answers.

Your bariatric surgeon owns anything anatomical or surgical: pain, obstruction, reflux that won't quit, hernias, complications, and any question about the mechanics of the procedure itself. Also the person to ask about revision surgery if that ever enters the conversation.

Your dietitian owns intake: protein targets, food tolerance, supplement timing, deficiencies, meal structure. This is the provider most people underuse most severely. Many programs include dietitian visits you're already entitled to and never book.

Your primary care doctor owns the whole picture: medications that may need adjusting as your weight changes, blood pressure, diabetes management, cholesterol, and general labs. They're also the one who coordinates when something falls between specialties.

A mental health provider owns everything Chapter 22 covers: transfer addiction, food anxiety, body image, and the emotional weight of a change this size. Some programs include this, and many people never take it up.

Knowing who owns what means your questions land with the person who can actually answer them, instead of getting a shrug from someone outside their lane.

What to Actually Bring to an Appointment

Appointments are short and your memory is worse than you think in the room. Walk in with something written.

  • Your real activity level, not a vague estimate. If you started lifting, say so. If your schedule changed, say so.
  • Any pattern lasting more than a week or two. Plateaus, energy crashes, cravings, new food intolerances, sleep changes.
  • Anything that scared you, even if it turned out to be nothing. A scary week is worth a five minute conversation.
  • A direct question about whether your current targets still make sense for where you are now, not where you were at your first post-op visit.
  • Your actual numbers, if you track them. A month of logged data answers questions no verbal summary can.

Questions Worth Asking Out Loud

Most people ask "am I doing okay?" and get "you're doing great," which is warm and useless. Specific questions get specific answers.

  • What should my protein target be given that I train three days a week?
  • Which labs should I be getting, and how often, for my specific procedure?
  • What symptoms would make you want to hear from me immediately, versus at my next visit?
  • Is this plateau consistent with where I am, or does it warrant a closer look?
  • What does maintenance look like for someone with my history, and how will we know when I'm in it?
  • Are there supplements I should be on for life, and are we monitoring for the deficiencies they prevent?

The Long-Term Labs Conversation

One thing that quietly slips as appointments space out: routine bloodwork. Depending on your procedure, nutrient absorption may be permanently altered, and deficiencies can develop slowly enough that you feel fine right up until you don't.

This is not a section where we tell you which labs to get or how often, because that genuinely varies by procedure, by program, and by person. It's a section telling you to ask, explicitly, and then to actually keep the appointments. Ask your team to write down the schedule. Put it in your calendar the way you'd put in a car inspection.

The people who run into trouble years out are rarely the ones who got bad advice. They're the ones who stopped showing up to get any.

When You Disagree With Your Provider

It happens. Here's the escalation path that keeps you safe and still gets you heard.

First, make sure they have the full picture. Most disagreements dissolve once the provider knows something they didn't, usually about your activity level, your symptoms, or your actual daily intake.

Second, ask for the reasoning. "Help me understand why that number" is not a challenge, it's a request. A good provider will explain. If the explanation makes sense, you've learned something. If it doesn't, you've learned something else.

Third, ask for a referral. Specialists exist. A general dietitian and a sports dietitian will genuinely give different answers to an active patient, and asking for the second opinion is a completely normal request.

Fourth, get a second opinion outright if it's significant and unresolved. This is standard medical practice, not an insult to anyone.

What isn't on that list: silently doing your own thing and not telling anyone. That's the one move that turns a manageable disagreement into a real risk.

Chapter 16: Why "Full" Gets Confusing at Year 2+

This chapter is the reason this book is called what it's called. If you only deeply absorb one chapter in the whole thing, make it this one.

Here's a question almost nobody asks out loud: what does "full" actually mean to you now? Not what it meant before surgery, when full usually meant something closer to "structurally unable to add one more bite." Not what it meant in month two, when your new pouch was so small that full arrived almost embarrassingly fast. What does it mean now, a year or two out, once your capacity has settled into whatever its long term normal is going to be?

Most people never actually answer that question. They keep eating off the old signal, or the month two signal, because nobody ever told them the signal itself needed an update.

Full Is a Signal, Not a Portion

That sentence is the whole thesis of this book, so it's worth unpacking properly.

A portion is a fixed amount of food. A signal is information your body sends about its current state. We're trained, most of us for decades, to eat portions: the plate, the serving, the amount that's in front of us, the amount that used to fill us. Portions are external. They live on the plate.

A signal is internal, and it varies. What satisfies you on a rest day is not what satisfies you after a hard training session. What satisfies you when you've slept eight hours is not what satisfies you when you've slept four. What satisfies you at month two is not what satisfies you at year two.

Surgery gives you a much louder signal for a while. That's genuinely the mechanism, and it's why the first year works almost automatically. The signal is so loud you can't ignore it. The mistake almost everyone makes is assuming the volume stays that high forever. It doesn't. Over time the signal gets quieter, subtler, and easier to talk yourself past. What replaces the volume, if anything is going to, is attention.

The Four Versions of Full You Will Meet

Most people cycle through recognizable stages, and mistaking one for another is where the trouble starts.

Pre-surgery full. Usually stretched, delayed, and defined by the plate rather than the body. For many of us this signal had been unreliable for years or decades before we ever got to an operating room.

Early post-op full. Extremely loud, arrives fast, and is often uncomfortable rather than satisfying. This is the phase where you learn the limit by occasionally crossing it, which is a memorable teacher.

Honeymoon full. Somewhere in the first year, capacity settles a little and the signal becomes more comfortable and more predictable. This is the phase most people mistake for permanent.

Year two and beyond full. Quieter. More like a suggestion than a stop sign. Real capacity has usually increased somewhat, which is normal and expected, and the loud enforcement mechanism you'd been relying on has softened. This is where the actual skill of eating to a signal has to be learned, because it's no longer being enforced for you.

Nobody warns you that the fourth stage is a different skill than the third. It is.

The Lag Nobody Accounts For

There's a delay built into the system that trips up almost everyone. Your stomach knows it's full before your brain gets the memo, and the gap between those two events is meaningful.

That gap used to be forgiving. When your capacity was large, a few extra bites during the lag barely registered. Post-surgery, the same lag can mean the difference between comfortable and genuinely miserable, because the margin for error got so much smaller while the delay stayed the same length.

Which leads to the single most practical mechanic in this chapter: speed is the variable you actually control. You cannot make the signal arrive faster. You can absolutely eat slowly enough that it arrives before you've gone past it.

This is why the classic advice, put the fork down between bites, is not a nagging platitude. It is the direct, mechanical countermeasure to a timing problem. Slowing down doesn't change your capacity. It changes whether you find out where your capacity is before or after you've exceeded it.

Recalibrating: A Practical Method

Treat your fullness signal like the mirrors in a car you drive every day. You adjusted them once. You still glance and adjust occasionally, because things shift.

Run a deliberate check once a quarter. Pick a low-stakes meal, at home, no screens, no rush. Eat slowly and pay attention to the exact moment satisfaction arrives, and note what it feels like now. Not what it felt like last year. Now.

Learn your own tells. For most people the true signal is not a dramatic sensation. It's subtle: a small loss of interest in the food, a change in how the next bite sounds in your head, a slight pressure. Learn yours specifically. Vague awareness of "full" is not the same as knowing your own signal.

Notice the difference between satisfied and stuffed. These are different states with different signals, and post-surgery the distance between them is short. Aiming for satisfied and stopping there is the entire game.

Check whether your context changed. More training, less sleep, a stressful stretch, new medication. All of these can shift what your body is asking for. A signal that seems wrong is often a signal responding correctly to something you haven't accounted for.

Distinguishing Hunger From Everything Else

Post-surgery, physical hunger often gets quieter, which paradoxically makes it easier to confuse with other things. A few distinctions worth having language for.

Physical hunger builds gradually, is open to a range of foods, and is satisfied by eating.

Head hunger arrives suddenly, is usually specific ("I want exactly this"), and is often attached to a mood, a time of day, or a situation rather than an actual need.

Thirst masquerades as hunger constantly, and post-surgery, when fluid intake is spread thin across the day by necessity, this happens more, not less.

Habit is the 9pm signal that has nothing to do with your body and everything to do with the fact that you have eaten at 9pm for eleven years.

None of these are moral categories. They're just different inputs, and being able to name which one is talking is most of what it takes to respond to it well.

Why This Chapter Is the Whole Book

Almost every other chapter is downstream of this one. Regain happens when the signal goes unread for long enough. Plateaus get misread when the signal isn't trusted. Travel goes sideways when the signal gets drowned out by novelty and someone else's schedule. Emotional eating is what happens when a different signal borrows food's phone.

Learning to read your own fullness signal, and re-learning it periodically as it changes, is the single most durable skill available to you here. The tool gave you a louder signal for a while. Reading it after it quiets down is the part that's yours.

Chapter 17: Protein-First, Revisited

You heard about protein constantly in year one. It was probably the single most repeated word from your entire care team, right alongside "hydrate" and "chew slowly." What almost nobody tells you is that your protein needs don't freeze in place the day your appointments start spacing out. They keep moving, especially if you start adding real activity into your life the way we did.

This is general education, not a prescription. Your actual target is a conversation with your own dietitian, not a number in this book, for the exact reason we walked through in the last chapter: the same generic target that undersells an active person can oversell a less active one, and neither version helps you if it's not actually built around your real life.

Why Protein Earns the Front of the Plate

Three reasons get repeated in bariatric care, and they're worth understanding rather than just obeying.

Muscle preservation. During rapid weight loss, the body will take some of what it needs from lean tissue as well as fat. Adequate protein is the main lever, alongside resistance training, that shifts the balance toward keeping muscle. Muscle you keep is muscle you don't have to rebuild later, and it's a meaningful part of what your metabolism does all day.

Satiety. Protein tends to be the most satisfying macronutrient per unit of volume, which matters enormously when your total volume is limited. When capacity is the constraint, what you spend it on becomes a real decision.

Healing and maintenance. Post-surgical recovery, hair, skin, and general repair all draw on protein availability, particularly in the first year.

The practical consequence of limited capacity is that eating order matters. If protein goes last, it often doesn't go at all, because you fill up before you get there. Protein-first isn't a preference, it's a scheduling decision imposed by a smaller container.

Distributing It Across the Day

The pattern that worked for us, offered as an example rather than a target, is that protein shows up early and often instead of once heroically at dinner.

Looking across our own logs, nearly every meal and snack has a protein source attached: eggs or deli turkey in the morning, a shake mid-morning, lean meat at lunch, a protein-forward afternoon snack, a lighter protein source at dinner. Cottage cheese, Greek-style yogurt, and a whey shake blended with milk and banana do a lot of repeat work.

The reason to spread it out isn't mystical. With limited capacity per sitting, hitting a meaningful daily total in two meals is arithmetically hard. Five or six smaller protein touchpoints get there without any single sitting being uncomfortable.

Sources That Tend to Work, and Ones That Fight Back

Tolerance is deeply individual and changes over time, so treat this as a starting map, not a rulebook.

Often well tolerated: eggs, fish and shellfish, ground meats, slow-cooked or stewed cuts, cottage cheese, Greek-style yogurt, cheese, tofu, protein shakes.

Frequently difficult, especially early: dry chicken breast, steak that isn't tender, tough or fibrous cuts, dense bread-adjacent items. Texture and moisture matter more than the protein source itself for a lot of people.

The shake question. Shakes are a tool, not a moral failure. They're also not a permanent substitute for food. Most programs want you moving toward food-based protein over time, with shakes filling gaps. Ask yours where they stand.

What Changes When You Start Training

This is the section that most first-year-oriented material simply doesn't have, and it's where a lot of active post-op patients quietly get underserved.

Resistance training changes the calculation in a few ways. Protein needs generally increase when you're actively trying to build or preserve muscle. Timing around workouts starts to matter more than it did when you were sedentary. And total energy intake becomes a real question, because trying to build muscle while in a significant deficit is a genuine tension, not something you can wish away.

None of that means you should pick your own number off the internet. It means you should book the appointment, describe your training accurately, and ask for a target built for that. If your program's dietitian doesn't work with active patients often, ask for a referral to someone who does. That's a completely reasonable request and specialists exist.

Common Traps

Protein at the end of the day. Waking up, eating lightly, and trying to make up an entire daily target after 6pm. Capacity says no.

Counting protein that's mostly something else. Some bars and drinks marketed as protein products deliver modest protein alongside substantial sugar. Read the actual panel.

Assuming more is automatically better. Protein needs are real but they aren't infinite, and for people with certain kidney or liver conditions, high intake is genuinely a medical question. This is one more reason the number comes from your provider rather than a book.

Letting protein crowd out everything else. Fiber, produce, and fluids still matter. Protein-first means first, not only.

Freezing the target permanently. The single most common error. Your target at month three, month eighteen, and year three may reasonably differ, and nobody will proactively tell you to revisit it.

Protein-first isn't a phase you graduate out of. It's a habit that needs occasional recalibration, same as everything else in this book.

Chapter 18: Hydration, Habits, and the Small Stuff That Slips

Nobody regains fifty pounds in a day. They regain it in a hundred invisible little quits, spread out so thin that no single one of them ever feels like a decision. This chapter is about catching them while they're still small.

Hydration Is a Timing Problem, Not a Willpower Problem

Hydration is the easiest habit to let slip, and the reason is mechanical rather than motivational. A smaller pouch makes drinking around meals a genuine balancing act. Most programs ask patients to separate fluids from meals by a window on either side, because filling limited space with liquid crowds out food, and because it can push food through faster than you want.

What that means practically is that your drinking window is chopped into segments by every meal and snack you eat. Someone eating five or six times a day has substantially less uninterrupted drinking time than they realize, which is why "just drink more water" fails as advice. The fix is structural.

  • Sip continuously during the windows you do have rather than trying to catch up in bursts
  • Keep a bottle within arm's reach at all times, because visibility drives intake more than intention does
  • Use the gaps deliberately: mid-morning, mid-afternoon, and evening are usually the longest uninterrupted stretches
  • Watch for the early tells of falling behind, which are usually fatigue, headache, and constipation before they are thirst
  • Remember that carbonation, caffeine, and alcohol all come with their own considerations post-surgery, and your program likely has a position on each

Ask your team what separation window and what daily target they want for you specifically. Those numbers vary by procedure and by person.

Supplements Are the Habit Most Likely to Quietly Die

Depending on your procedure, nutrient absorption may be permanently altered, which makes supplementation a lifelong requirement rather than a recovery-phase chore. It is also, by a wide margin, the habit people abandon first, because nothing bad happens the day you stop. Deficiencies develop slowly and silently, and the feedback loop is so delayed that skipping feels consequence-free for months.

Treat this as infrastructure, not discipline. Systems beat memory.

  • Attach the habit to something you already do without fail, like coffee or brushing your teeth
  • Use a weekly pill organizer so a missed day is visible rather than theoretical
  • Reorder on a recurring schedule before you run out, since the gap between "ran out" and "stopped taking them" is usually about four days
  • Keep the labs appointments, because bloodwork is the only thing that actually tells you whether what you're doing is working

The Mechanics of Eating That Erode First

Three habits from early recovery tend to fade around the same time, usually without anyone noticing.

Chewing thoroughly. Early on this is enforced by immediate discomfort. As tolerance improves, the enforcement weakens and speed creeps back in.

Putting the fork down between bites. The most effective and most abandoned technique in the entire post-op toolkit. It exists to solve the timing lag from Chapter 16, and it stops working the moment you stop doing it.

Eating without a screen. Distracted eating reliably outpaces the fullness signal, because attention is the entire mechanism by which you notice a quiet signal.

None of these are moral issues. They're all just speed governors, and speed is the variable that determines whether you find your limit before or after you cross it.

The Drift Audit

Here is a practical exercise worth running quarterly. It takes ten minutes and catches most problems while they're still small.

Ask yourself, honestly, about each of these:

  • Am I still tracking anything, even loosely, or did that quietly stop?
  • Am I still taking supplements daily, or is it more like most days?
  • When did I last have bloodwork done?
  • Has my eating speed crept up?
  • Am I still eating protein first, or has it drifted to whatever's convenient?
  • Have my portions grown gradually? Would last year's portion look small to me now?
  • Am I drinking what I used to drink, or has that slipped?
  • Am I still moving the way I was six months ago?
  • When did I last weigh myself or take a measurement?
  • Am I avoiding any of these questions? Which one, and why?

That last question is the most useful one on the list. Avoidance is a reliable signal, and it usually points directly at the thing that's drifted furthest.

Why Small Beats Dramatic

The instinct after spotting drift is to overhaul everything at once. Resist it. Overhauls are motivated by guilt, run on adrenaline, and expire in about eleven days.

Pick one habit. Rebuild it for two weeks until it's automatic again. Then pick the next one. This is slower than a dramatic reset and it is the only version that survives past the first hard week.

The small stuff is small precisely because it can be fixed cheaply, if you catch it. That's the entire argument for looking.

Notice the shape of it more than the specifics: protein shows up at nearly every single meal and snack, not just at lunch and dinner. That's the actual pattern, not the exact foods.

A Birthday, Handled Honestly

Here's a day we're including on purpose, because a book that only shows the perfect days is lying to you. This was a birthday dinner out, wings, onion rings, tortilla chips, strawberry lemonade, the whole thing. We logged every bit of it, the same way we logged everything else. No separate secret notebook for the "bad" days. It's just data, not a confession.

The next day, we went right back to the regular pattern. No punishment meal, no compensating by starving. Just back to normal, because one dinner out doesn't erase months of consistency, and it doesn't need to be treated like it does.

A Training Day

On gym days, the shake timing shifts around the workout instead of around the clock. One logged day had a pre-gym breakfast of light bread, banana, peanut butter, and an electrolyte drink, then a post-gym shake with whey, whole milk, and banana. The point isn't the specific shake recipe. It's that protein showed up deliberately around the workout, not as an afterthought.

The Ingredients That Showed Up Constantly

Not a meal plan, just the staples that kept reappearing across weeks of logs, in case any of it sparks an idea for your own list:

  • Cortado or cappuccino to start the day

  • Cottage cheese, Greek-style yogurt, and protein shakes as snack anchors

  • Lean proteins at lunch and dinner (chicken, beef, shrimp, pork)

  • Fresh fruit worked in throughout the day, not treated as off-limits

  • Whey protein blended with whole milk and banana as a reliable go-to shake

Swap every single one of these for whatever fits your own allergies, preferences, and your team's actual recommendations. The pattern is the point. The grocery list isn't.

When the Body Says Stop, Actually Stop

One more thing worth including here, straight from the same tracker. During a recent workout, a sharp pain showed up, an 8 out of 10, and it got logged exactly like that, flagged in plain language: needs medical evaluation, not just a workaround. That week off from the gym wasn't a failure of discipline. It was the correct call. The tracker isn't just for celebrating wins. It's for catching the moment your body is telling you something that a swapped exercise or a lighter set isn't going to fix, the moment where the actual answer is your phone and your doctor's number, not your gym app.

Chapter 20: Building Your Own Rhythm Instead of Copying Someone Else's

If you read Chapter 19 and immediately felt the urge to copy our exact meals, cortado and all, we understand the instinct, and we're asking you to resist it anyway. Not because there's anything wrong with a cortado. Because the specific foods were never the point. The pattern underneath them is the actual thing worth taking.

Why Copying Someone Else's Plan Fails

Borrowed plans fail for reasons that have nothing to do with discipline.

Your schedule isn't theirs. We work from home. That single fact shapes our entire eating pattern, and it makes our rhythm nearly useless to someone working a twelve hour shift, driving a route, or teaching a classroom where eating happens on a bell.

Your tolerances aren't theirs. Post-surgery food tolerance is unpredictable and individual. Half of a borrowed plan may be food you physically don't do well with.

Your preferences aren't theirs. This is the one people dismiss and shouldn't. A plan full of food you don't enjoy has a shelf life measured in weeks. Enjoyment isn't a luxury here, it's the load-bearing wall of adherence.

Your phase isn't theirs. A year two plan handed to a month two patient is genuinely inappropriate, and possibly unsafe. Your program's staged progression exists for a reason.

Your medical picture isn't theirs. Allergies, medications, comorbidities, procedure type. All of it changes the answer.

The pattern transfers. The plan doesn't.

Step One: Map the Day You Actually Have

Start with your real schedule, not an idealized one. If mornings are chaos in your house, a leisurely three-course breakfast isn't a realistic anchor no matter how good it looked in someone else's chapter.

Write down, honestly: when you wake up, when you leave, when you have genuine breaks, when you get home, when you sleep. Then mark which of those windows can realistically hold food, and which can only hold something quick.

Most people build a plan for the life they wish they had, then blame themselves when the life they actually have won't accommodate it. Start from the real one.

Step Two: Choose Two or Three Protein Anchors

An anchor is a protein source you genuinely like, tolerate reliably, and can produce with almost no effort or decision-making. For us it's shakes and cottage cheese. For you it might be eggs, Greek yogurt, deli turkey, or leftover chicken you'll happily eat cold out of the fridge at 10am with no judgment from anyone.

Two or three is the right number. Fewer and you burn out on them. More and you've reintroduced decision fatigue, which is the actual enemy.

The test for an anchor: could you eat this three times a week for the next year without dreading it, and can you get it into your hand in under two minutes on a bad day? If yes, it's an anchor.

Step Three: Build In Flexibility on Purpose, Before You Need It

We showed you a birthday dinner in Chapter 19, logged honestly, followed by a completely normal next day. That wasn't an accident or a slip. It was a rule we'd already set: nothing is off limits forever, and no single meal gets treated as a crisis.

Decide your own version of that rule now, while you're calm, instead of improvising it in the middle of an actual holiday dinner while everyone's watching what's on your plate.

Some versions worth considering:

  • Nothing is permanently forbidden, but nothing is unlogged either
  • One planned indulgent meal a week, chosen in advance rather than stumbled into
  • Special occasions are exempt, and the next meal returns to normal without compensation
  • No punishment meals, no skipped meals to "make up for" anything

The specific rule matters far less than having decided it before you're standing in front of the dessert table with everyone watching.

Step Four: Write It Down and Test It for Two Weeks

Your rhythm should fit on an index card. Something like: protein at every meal and snack, three anchors on rotation, eating windows at these five times, fluids in these three gaps, one flexible meal per week.

Then run it for two weeks without changing anything, and pay attention to where it broke. It will break somewhere. That's information, not failure.

Common breakage points and what they usually mean:

The 4pm collapse. Usually means lunch was too light or too early, or there's no anchor positioned in the afternoon.

Weekend drift. Almost always a structure problem, not a willpower problem. Weekends have different shapes and usually need their own version of the plan.

Evening grazing. Frequently traces back to insufficient protein earlier in the day, and sometimes to habit or emotion rather than hunger, which Chapter 22 covers.

Anchor fatigue. You stopped wanting the thing. Rotate in a replacement, don't force it.

Adjust one variable at a time, then run another two weeks. This is unglamorous and it's how a rhythm actually gets built.

When You Share a Kitchen

Cooking one meal for a household with different needs is its own skill, and it gets easier with a component approach.

Build meals from separable parts: a protein, a vegetable, a starch, a sauce. Everyone assembles their own plate from the same components in the proportions they need. Nobody cooks two dinners, nobody eats sad diet food while watching someone else eat dinner, and the person post-surgery simply takes a different ratio of the same ingredients.

If your partner is also post-op, as ours was for each other, you get the added benefit described in Chapter 21: shared plates, smaller grocery bills, and a built-in accountability partner who doesn't need anything explained to them.

If your household isn't on board at all, that's a real difficulty and it's covered in Chapter 29.

The Only Test That Matters

Your rhythm is going to look different from ours, and it should. The measure of whether it's working isn't whether it matches this book. It's whether you can still be doing it a year from now without gritting your teeth.

If the honest answer is no, the plan is wrong. Not you.

Chapter 21: Eating Out, Travel, and Holidays Without the Spiral

You already know how this one goes for us, because you read about it in Chapter 4. A trip abroad to visit family, moderation that quietly stopped meaning moderation, and 12 pounds gained in a single month. We're not going to repeat the whole story here, but we are going to pull the actual lessons out of it, because travel and holidays are exactly the terrain where all the good habits you've built get tested at once, usually in someone else's kitchen, on someone else's schedule, surrounded by people who love you and have no idea they're testing anything.

The first lesson is that travel removes your usual structure all at once, not gradually. Your normal meal timing, your usual protein anchors, your regular tracking habit, all of it can disappear in the same week, which means several small supports fail simultaneously instead of one at a time. Knowing that in advance lets you pick one or two things to protect on purpose, rather than watching everything slip and being surprised by it.

The second lesson is that "just this once" math doesn't actually work the way it feels like it should in the moment. A single indulgent meal genuinely is just one meal. A week of "just this once" meals stacked back to back is not one meal anymore, it's a new pattern wearing a "just this once" costume, and it deserves to be recognized as one.

The third lesson is that prevention and recovery are two different skills, and this chapter is about prevention. If the trip already got away from you and you're reading this from the other side of it, skip ahead to Chapter 24, that's the full recovery playbook, step by step, exactly as we ran it.

The Restaurant Math Nobody Warned Us About

Here is a genuine plot twist neither of us saw coming: eating out got cheaper. Substantially cheaper. Not because we stopped going out, we go out plenty, but because two post-op adults ordering separate entrees is now an almost comical waste of food and money.

We share nearly everything. One entree between us, sometimes with a side or an appetizer added, and both of us walk out satisfied. The first time we did it we felt vaguely like we were getting away with something, sitting there splitting one plate like a couple of teenagers on a budget date. Then the check came and we realized the teenagers had the right idea the whole time.

Run the actual math on your own life and it gets almost funny. A dinner out that used to mean two entrees, maybe two appetizers, and a couple of drinks, now means one entree, one shared side, and water, and the bill drops by more than half. Multiply that across a year of ordinary date nights, birthdays, and the nights nobody feels like cooking, and it stops being a cute observation and starts being real money.

Our Actual Ordering Strategy

We didn't invent this on purpose. We built it by trial and error, mostly by ordering wrong a few dozen times and paying attention. Here's what it looks like now.

Order the protein-forward entree, not the carb-forward one. Between the pasta and the steak, the steak wins every time, because it is the item that will actually satisfy two people who fill up fast. The pasta will still be sitting there when you're both done, wondering what it did wrong.

Ask for the box when the food arrives, not at the end. This one sounds small and it changes everything. We split the plate right away, portion what we're actually going to eat, and box the rest before either of us takes a first bite. Deciding to stop while you're already full is a coin flip. Deciding before you start is just a decision.

Skip the appetizer, or make the appetizer the meal. Some nights the appetizer is genuinely the more interesting item on the menu and it's exactly the right size for us. Ordering an appetizer and a shared entree is where the math quietly goes sideways.

Order to the shared plate, not to your individual craving. This is the one that took actual practice, and it turns out it's less a food skill than a marriage skill, which brings us to the real point of this section.

One Plate, One Suitcase

Here's the thing about sharing an entree with someone you love: somebody is going to have to give up their first choice. Every single time. That's not a downside of sharing, it's the entire mechanism.

We think about our marriage the way you'd think about a trip with one shared suitcase.

Before, we each had our own luggage. Two bags, two sets of preferences, no negotiation needed, and no real reason to think about what the other person was packing. Now there's one bag between us, and every trip requires the same conversation: how much space does each of us actually need this time?

Some trips split down the middle, an even fifty-fifty, and nobody has to think about it. Some trips she needs more room, because it's her family, her event, her week that's been harder than mine. Some trips I need more room, and she gives it without keeping score. The bag doesn't get bigger. What changes is who's being generous, and when.

Ordering one plate is that exact conversation, three or four times a week, in miniature. Tonight I want the short rib and she wants the salmon, and one of us is going to say "let's do yours," and it will not be the same one of us every time. If it is always the same one of us, that's not a food problem, that's a much older problem wearing a menu as a disguise.

What surprised us is how good this has been for our marriage in general. We practice small generosity constantly now, in a low-stakes setting, over dinner. You get better at the big version by rehearsing the small one. Twenty-two years in, we're better at yielding to each other than we were at year ten, and a nontrivial amount of the credit goes to a restaurant table and a single shared entree.

Better Food, Smaller Bill

The other thing that changed, and this one genuinely surprised us, is the grocery bill. It went down. Not because we started buying cheap food. Because we started buying good food, in much smaller quantities.

When you're eating volume, quality gets expensive fast, so you compromise. You buy the larger, cheaper cut. You buy the family-size everything. You optimize for filling people up. When your actual intake drops, that entire calculation flips. Six ounces of genuinely excellent beef costs less than a pound and a half of the mediocre stuff, and it is a dramatically better dinner.

So now we buy the good olive oil. The real Parmigiano. The better cut of meat, the fresher fish, the produce that actually tastes like something. For two people who grew up in kitchens where nobody was ever allowed to leave hungry, this was the unexpected consolation prize: we eat less, and we eat considerably better than we used to.

There's a quiet lesson buried in that. A lot of us spent years associating "eating well" with abundance, with the full table and the second helping and the leftovers that feed you Tuesday. It turns out you can trade quantity for quality and end up feeling richer, not poorer. Our grocery cart is smaller now and there's genuinely nothing sad in it.

Practically, Going Into Your Next Trip or Holiday Season

  • Pick your one or two non-negotiables in advance. Protein at most meals and some form of daily movement work well as defaults.
  • Give yourself explicit permission for real indulgence rather than a guilty, secretive version of it. The guilty version tends to spiral further than an honest one does.
  • If you're traveling with a partner who's also post-op, plan to share. It is cheaper, it is less food waste, and it forces exactly the kind of small negotiation that keeps you both honest.
  • Decide, before you leave, that a hard week doesn't require punishment when you get back. It just requires you to start again, the same day you notice.

Chapter 22: The Emotional Triggers Nobody Warns You About

◆

Nobody sits you down before surgery and tells you this part, but it's real enough that it has an actual clinical name: transfer addiction. The theory is simple enough to explain in one sentence and complicated enough to catch almost everyone off guard. When food stops being available as a coping mechanism, because your new pouch physically will not let you overeat your way through a bad day anymore, the compulsion underneath the eating does not just vanish. It goes looking for a new job.

The most common new hire, by a wide margin, is alcohol. And if you're picking a replacement vice, alcohol is genuinely the worst possible candidate for a bariatric patient, for reasons that have nothing to do with willpower. It's calorie dense in a way that sneaks past your usual defenses, and it does not fill your pouch the way solid food does, so none of the natural "okay, that's enough" signals show up to stop you. You can drink far more than you'd ever be able to eat, and your body won't tap you on the shoulder to tell you when to quit. That's a dangerous combination on top of a tool that was supposed to help you control intake in the first place.

We got lucky here, honestly. Neither of us was ever much of a drinker, so alcohol was never a door that was open to walk through. Truth be told, we've never even been drunk, not once, in our entire lives together. My personal theory is that some deep, self-preserving part of my brain has always known that if I ever actually got drunk, I would strip down and go streaking through the neighborhood like a man reenacting a college fraternity legend nobody asked for. Renata's version of the same fear is far more sensible: she just really, really did not want the hangover headache. Neither of those is why we never drank, honestly. We just were never into it in the first place. But if you're looking for two wildly different reasons to stay sober, we've got you covered.

Our actual weakness, before surgery, was good food. Big portions. Constant snacking. That was our whole game, and it was a familiar one.

What surprised us was watching a new door open after surgery instead of an old one reopening. We both noticed, separately at first and then very obviously together, a pattern creeping in around buying clothes. Not "treat yourself occasionally" shopping. A compulsive pull toward it, one that had the same shape as the old food compulsion, just wearing a different outfit. Pun intended, and yes, that one's clearly a joke, not a diagnosis.

Once we noticed it, we did the only thing that's ever actually worked for either of us: we called it out to each other, out loud, every time we saw the pattern showing up. Not as an accusation. As a "hey, I see what this is" check-in. I tried something else too, on purpose: instead of just trying to suppress the compulsive energy, I tried redirecting it somewhere useful. Exercise became the place I sent that same restless, compulsive energy when it showed up. It didn't erase the impulse. It just gave the impulse a job that builds something instead of one that empties a bank account or a pantry.

If you take one thing from this section, let it be this: the compulsion doesn't disappear just because the food access does. It's looking for a new address. Your job isn't to be shocked when it finds one. Your job is to notice fast, say it out loud to someone who'll actually hold you accountable, and if you can, point that same energy at something that builds you up instead of something that quietly costs you. And if what you're noticing in yourself feels bigger than a habit, closer to something you can't step back from on your own, that's exactly the kind of thing to bring to your care team, not something to white-knuckle through alone.

Chapter 23: Plateaus vs. Regain, How to Tell the Difference

You already watched this happen in Chapter 4: ten days stuck at 250 pounds, every old alarm bell ringing at once, my brain drafting the regain story before anything had actually gone wrong. What got me through those ten days wasn't willpower. It was having something I never had during the old 260-pound standoffs: an actual way to tell the difference between a normal plateau and the early signs of real regain, instead of treating every stall as the same emergency.

Why Plateaus Happen at All

A plateau is not a malfunction. It's usually the body doing something reasonable, and knowing which reasonable thing is happening takes most of the fear out of it.

Water and glycogen shifts. These can mask real fat loss for days or weeks at a time. Sodium, carbohydrate intake, hormonal cycles, a new training block, poor sleep, and stress all move water weight independently of fat.

Body recomposition. If you're training, you may be adding muscle while losing fat. The scale can hold completely still while your actual body composition improves substantially. This was my entire second year.

Genuine metabolic adaptation. A smaller body burns fewer calories at rest. This is arithmetic, not sabotage, and it means the intake that produced loss at a heavier weight may produce maintenance at a lighter one.

Unnoticed intake creep. Portions grow gradually. Tracking gets loose. Bites, licks, and tastes stop getting counted. This is the one worth being honest about, because it's the most common and the most fixable.

Approaching your actual set point. At some stage, the body is close to where it's going to settle, and progress genuinely slows.

The Distinguishing Questions

Here's the checklist worth running before you panic.

Signs this is probably a normal plateau:

  • The number has stalled, but your habits haven't changed. You're still eating and moving the way you were before the stall started.
  • It's been a few days to a couple of weeks, not months.
  • Your clothes and your energy haven't noticeably shifted, even though the scale has stopped moving.
  • You can point to a plausible, boring explanation: water retention, a harder training block, hormonal timing, a salty week.
  • Measurements or strength numbers are still moving even though weight isn't.

Signs worth actually flagging to your care team:

  • The stall has lasted well beyond a couple of weeks with no movement at all.
  • It's paired with a real, identifiable shift in your own habits: portions creeping up, activity dropping off, tracking that quietly stopped.
  • Clothes fitting differently, energy dropping, or other physical changes are showing up alongside the number.
  • The scale is moving upward consistently over several weeks, not fluctuating.
  • You genuinely can't tell whether this is normal, and the not knowing itself is causing you real distress.

That last point matters more than it might seem. If the uncertainty is eating at you, that's reason enough to make the call, even if it turns out to be nothing. Nobody's dietitian has ever been annoyed by a patient checking in too early.

Measure Something Other Than Weight

The single most useful thing you can do during a plateau is stop relying on one number that's currently refusing to speak to you.

  • Measurements. Waist, chest, hips, arms, thighs. These often keep moving during recomposition when weight doesn't.
  • How clothes fit. Unglamorous and surprisingly accurate.
  • Strength numbers. If you're training, a logbook is a progress record the scale can't see.
  • Photos. Same lighting, same position, same time of day, monthly. Visual change is invisible day to day and obvious across eight weeks.
  • Energy, sleep, and labs. Health markers frequently improve on their own schedule regardless of what the scale is doing.

A plateau on every single one of these metrics simultaneously is a different situation than a plateau on weight alone. That's exactly the distinction you want data for.

The Two Week Protocol

If you're stalled and want a structured response rather than a panicked one, here's a reasonable one.

Week one: measure, don't change. Track everything honestly, including the bites you'd normally skip logging. Take measurements. Change nothing else. Most of the time this alone reveals the answer, and often the answer is "intake crept and I didn't notice."

Week two: adjust one variable. If week one showed drift, correct that one thing. If it showed nothing, look at sleep, stress, or hydration before touching food. Change one thing so you can tell what worked.

Then reassess. If nothing has moved after a genuine, honest two weeks, that's the point to bring real data to your dietitian rather than guessing further on your own.

What's not on the protocol: dramatic restriction, skipping meals, or punishing yourself. Those tend to produce a short scale drop followed by a bigger rebound, which is how a plateau turns into an actual regain cycle.

What Made the Difference For Us

Our own ten day plateau ended up firmly in the first category. The habits hadn't changed. The gym schedule hadn't changed. It was just a normal stall that resolved itself once the body caught up, which it did, right on schedule, once we stopped panicking long enough to let it.

The single biggest difference between this plateau and every one of my old ones wasn't the plateau itself. It was that this time, the habits underneath it never actually broke. Before surgery, the plateau caused the giving up, and the giving up caused the regain. The plateau was never really the problem. The response to it was.

Chapter 24: What We Did When We Slipped

You already know the big one. Twelve pounds in a month, a trip abroad, moderation that quietly stopped meaning anything. We told that story in full back in Chapter 4, so we won't repeat it here. What belongs in this chapter is the actual mechanics of what we did afterward, because "we got back on track" is the kind of sentence that sounds nice on a poster and is genuinely useless as actual guidance.

First, What a Slip Actually Is

A slip is a stretch where your habits came apart, and the weight followed. That's it. It is not a verdict on your character, and it is not the surgery failing.

It's worth separating three things that often get collapsed into one panic.

A bad meal is a single event. It is statistically meaningless against a month of eating and requires no response at all.

A bad week is a short pattern. It needs noticing and a return to baseline, nothing more dramatic than that.

A drift is a pattern that's been running long enough to become the new normal without anyone deciding it should be. This is the one worth actually working on, and it's the only one of the three that deserves a whole chapter.

Most people respond to the first as though it were the third, which is exactly how a bad meal becomes a bad month.

The Five Step Recovery

Here's what getting back on track actually looked like, step by step, not as inspiration but as a literal checklist you can borrow.

Step one: name it, out loud, without softening it. Not "I've been a little off lately." The actual number, the actual timeframe, said plainly to the other person, or written down if you're doing this alone. Vague language lets you keep avoiding the problem. Specific language makes it impossible to pretend it isn't real.

Step two: go back to tracking immediately, even though it's uncomfortable. The instinct after a slip is to avoid the scale and avoid the log, because looking feels like it'll make the guilt worse. It's the opposite. Not tracking just lets the slip continue invisibly. We picked the tracker back up the same week, not once we'd already fixed it, because the tracking is part of the fix, not a reward for finishing it.

Step three: return to the boring baseline, not a punishment version of it. We didn't crash diet our way out of twelve pounds. We went back to the exact rhythm that had worked for the year before, protein-first meals, the same gym schedule, nothing extreme in either direction. Punishment diets tend to create the next slip. Boring consistency is what actually closes the gap.

Step four: let the timeline be what it is. It took roughly three months to close most of that gap. Not three days, not three weeks. We didn't try to make up for a month of drift in a week, because that math doesn't work and chasing it usually just triggers another cycle of frustration and giving up.

Step five: turn it into information instead of shame. The slip taught us something real: unstructured travel with no guardrails is a genuine risk area for us specifically. That's useful. It's data we didn't have before, and it changed how we've approached every trip since. A slip you learn nothing from is just a bad month. A slip you extract a lesson from is basically a data collection expense.

The Traps That Turn a Slip Into a Spiral

The compensating fast. Skipping meals to make up for yesterday. This reliably produces excessive hunger later, which produces the next slip, which produces the next fast. It's the oldest loop there is.

Waiting for Monday. A slip on Wednesday does not require waiting five days to address. The delay is not a plan, it's avoidance wearing a calendar.

All-or-nothing thinking. "I've already blown it today" is the single most expensive sentence in weight management. One meal is one meal. The day is not a unit that can be ruined.

Hiding it. Not telling your partner, not logging it, not mentioning it at your appointment. Slips that stay secret stay unaddressed, and they're the ones that turn into drift.

Over-correcting into misery. Responding to a two-pound week with an unsustainable regimen you'll abandon in nine days, leaving you further behind than when you started.

The Delay-and-Fix Reframe

Here's the framing that got me through my own version, and it's worth sitting with.

A slip delays your progress. It does not erase it.

You still have the surgery. You still have the habits, even if they're currently dusty. You still have every piece of knowledge you've built about your own body. Twelve pounds on a scale does not undo eighteen months of learning, and the fact that I closed most of that gap by simply doing what I already knew how to do is the proof.

The tool didn't fail. I stopped using it correctly for a month, and the moment I started again, it started working again. That's not a comeback story. It's just how tools work.

If You're In One Right Now

Do exactly one thing today. Not the whole list.

Weigh yourself, or log one meal, or go to the gym once, or eat one protein-first breakfast. One thing, today, right now.

Momentum in this direction works the same way it does in the other one: it accumulates from things too small to feel significant. The point of doing one thing today is not the thing. It's ending the stretch where you were doing nothing, because that's the actual problem you're solving.

If you're in the middle of your own version of this right now, the timeline in this chapter is the part worth holding onto most. It gets better, but it gets better on its own schedule, not on the schedule your guilt is demanding.

Chapter 25: When Your Results Don't Look Like the Before-and-Afters

If you had the surgery, did the work, and ended up somewhere short of what you pictured, this chapter is for you. It's probably the most important one in this book, and it's the one almost nobody writes, because it doesn't make a good marketing photo.

We want to start with the thing that needs saying most plainly: losing less than you expected is not evidence that you failed. It is one of the most common outcomes of this surgery, and it is a medical reality long before it is ever a discipline problem.

The Numbers Nobody Puts On the Poster

Bariatric outcomes are usually described in terms of excess weight loss, meaning the portion of weight above a target range that a patient actually loses. The published averages sound impressive, and they are. But an average is the middle of a very wide range, and half of every average is made of people who landed below it.

Real outcomes vary enormously between patients who had the same procedure, from the same surgeon, following the same instructions. That variation is driven substantially by things nobody chooses:

  • Starting point and body composition. Two people who lose identical amounts can end up looking completely different.
  • Age and hormonal environment. Both meaningfully affect how bodies respond, and neither is a matter of effort.
  • Genetics and baseline metabolism. Some bodies defend weight harder than others. This is measurable and it is not a personality trait.
  • Medications. A number of common ones, including certain antidepressants, mood stabilizers, steroids, and diabetes drugs, are associated with weight retention or gain. If you take any of them, you are not fighting the same fight as someone who doesn't.
  • Medical conditions. Thyroid issues, PCOS, insulin resistance, and other conditions change the math.
  • Surgical variation. Technique, pouch and limb sizing, and how the anatomy healed differ from case to case, even for the same named procedure.

Notice how many of those are things you cannot see from the outside, cannot control, and would never show up in a before-and-after post.

The Before-and-After Problem

Here's why your expectations may have been calibrated wrong, through no fault of yours.

The photos you've seen are a filtered sample. People post dramatic results. People with modest results usually post nothing, quietly conclude they're the exception, and disappear from the conversation. Surgical practices display their best outcomes, which is not dishonest, it's advertising, and advertising selects for the extraordinary. Support groups over-represent the thriving and the struggling, and under-represent everyone in the middle.

So you built a mental picture of "normal" out of a highly unrepresentative sample, and then measured yourself against it.

For whatever it's worth, one of the two authors of this book is not at his goal either. I'm still cutting fat, still in a body recomposition phase that moves like a glacier, still four pounds from closing a gap I opened on a trip. Renata is further along. She's also, by a wide margin, the more disciplined of the two of us, and pretending otherwise wouldn't help anyone.

When Life Removes Your Structure

Here's a pattern worth naming, because it hits a lot of people and gets misread as a character flaw every single time.

Some people's lives have built-in structural cliffs. School employees hit a two-and-a-half month summer with no routine attached to it. Shift workers rotate. Seasonal workers get a slow season. Parents hit school breaks. Anyone can hit a job change, a move, an illness in the family, or a stretch of caregiving that erases every habit they had.

When the structure goes, several supports fail simultaneously. Meal timing loses its anchors. The exercise routine loses the schedule it was hanging on. Tracking loses its natural checkpoints. Sleep shifts. And because it all happens at once, it feels like a personal collapse rather than what it actually is: a predictable systems failure with a predictable cause.

If you gain during those stretches, the honest diagnosis is not "I lack willpower." It's "my routine was load-bearing and it was removed."

What actually helps is building a second version of your plan in advance, one specifically for the unstructured stretch:

  • Decide before the break starts what your two non-negotiables will be. Two, not ten.
  • Anchor eating to something that still exists in the new schedule, even if it's arbitrary. An alarm works.
  • Cut the exercise target rather than abandon it. Twenty minutes three times a week beats an intended hour that never happens.
  • Write down the date the structure returns, and treat it as a scheduled restart rather than a rescue.
  • Expect the first week back to feel terrible. It always does, and it always passes.

A cliff you see coming is a much smaller cliff.

Things Actually Worth Investigating

If your results are meaningfully below what your team projected, that deserves a medical conversation, not a private verdict about your character. Bring it to your surgeon and dietitian and ask directly:

  • Is there anything anatomical worth evaluating, and would imaging or an endoscopy be appropriate?
  • Are any of my current medications working against me, and are there alternatives?
  • Should we check thyroid function, insulin resistance, or other metabolic markers?
  • Are the anti-obesity medications now used alongside surgery an option for me?
  • What does revision surgery look like in my case, and would I be a candidate?
  • Can I be referred to a dietitian who specializes in patients further out from surgery?

That last one matters more than it sounds. Most bariatric nutrition support is built for the first year. Getting real support for year two and beyond often requires asking for it specifically.

And note the fourth question especially. The medical landscape has changed. Combining surgery with medication is now a legitimate, actively practiced approach, and needing it is not a confession of failure any more than needing the surgery was.

Putting Down the Blame

If you take one thing from this chapter, make it this.

You did not waste the surgery. Whatever you've lost, you've lost, and every pound of it is doing work on your blood pressure, your joints, your blood sugar, and your risk of the things that were coming for you. Health improvements from bariatric surgery show up at loss levels far below the dramatic transformations, and they are not consolation prizes. They were the actual point.

You are also not done. This is a chapter of the story, not the ending. People restart this at year two, year five, year ten, and it works, because the tool is still there and the tool does not expire.

And there is nobody keeping score. The imagined judge who is disappointed in you does not exist. Your surgeon isn't disappointed, they've seen the whole range. Your family isn't running the numbers. The only person holding you to a standard set by strangers' photographs is you, and you're allowed to stop.

Reread Chapter 26 when you can. The scoreboards in it are the honest ones, and most people in this position are winning on several of them without having noticed, because they're only looking at the one that isn't moving.

If you're stuck, the move is not more shame. It's one conversation with your care team and one small habit restarted this week. That's it. That's the whole assignment.

Chapter 26: Redefining Success Past the Number on the Scale

At some point the scale stops being an honest referee. If you keep letting it call the game anyway, it will rob you of wins you already earned. Here's what to score instead.

For most of this book, we've been fairly comfortable throwing around real numbers: pounds lost, plateaus, targets. This chapter is the one where we tell you those numbers matter less than you've been trained to think, especially the further out from surgery you get.

Why the Scale Becomes Unreliable

Body recomposition is the reason this chapter has to exist. Once you're trading fat for muscle instead of just losing weight outright, the scale becomes an increasingly unreliable narrator.

It can sit still for months while your actual body is doing real, visible work underneath. It can even go up slightly while you're objectively healthier and stronger than you were the month before. It cannot distinguish between fat, muscle, water, or the salt content of last night's dinner. It reports one number for four different variables and offers no breakdown.

If the scale is your only scoreboard, body recomposition will make you feel like you're failing during exactly the period you're succeeding the most. That's not a small psychological problem. That's the mechanism that has ended a lot of people's progress right at the moment it was working.

Six Scoreboards Worth Keeping

1. Health markers. This is the one that arguably matters most and gets tracked least. Blood pressure. A1C. Cholesterol panel. Medications reduced or discontinued. Sleep apnea improvement. These are the reasons most of us qualified for surgery in the first place, and they're measurable, objective, and reviewed by an actual professional. When the scale goes quiet, this is the scoreboard to look at.

2. Strength and capacity. What you can lift, how far you can walk, how many flights of stairs before you notice. A training log is a progress record the scale physically cannot see. Watching a number go up while your body weight holds steady is one of the more satisfying experiences available in year two.

3. Measurements and fit. Waist, chest, hips, arms, thighs. And the unglamorous, highly accurate one: how clothes fit. Recomposition shows up here long before it shows up on a scale.

4. Energy and sleep. How you feel at 3pm. Whether you wake up rested. Whether you have anything left after work. These are hard to quantify and impossible to fake, and most people's improvement here is dramatic and completely uncelebrated.

5. What you can now do. Hiking instead of waiting in the lobby. Keeping up with your kids. Fitting comfortably in an airplane seat. Getting off the floor without planning it. This category tends to hold the wins people actually remember years later.

6. How you move through the world. Walking into a store without dread. Sitting normally in a meeting instead of angling to take up less space. Being in photographs on purpose. This is the least measurable scoreboard and frequently the most meaningful.

Some of Ours

An eighteen year old calling her dad a gym rat, unprompted, because she'd actually noticed. A plateau that resolved in ten days instead of turning into a multi-year standoff. Clothes fitting differently during a month the scale barely moved. Hiking together instead of one of us guarding the bags. Blood pressure and A1C that stopped being a conversation topic at appointments. A shirt size that stopped being a negotiation.

None of those show up on a scale. All of them are real progress, and several of them mattered more to us than any single number ever did.

Here's one that still gets me. Back in 2019, I took our kids to Six Flags. I love roller coasters, and so do they. I didn't fit in the seats. The seatbelts would never close. Some rides had a "big and tall" option, and the amount of praying I did for that thing to click shut had more faith behind it than the whole Vatican combined. June 2025, a little less than a year after surgery, I took my kids to Six Flags again, on Father's Day this time. We made it a challenge, whoever chickened out first lost, and nobody did. As a family, we rode every single one. Renata spent the day holding the bags and everyone's phones, and to be clear, that had nothing to do with whether she'd fit. She's just always been afraid of roller coasters, and no surgery was ever going to fix that particular problem.

Building Your Own Scoreboard

Pick three metrics from the six categories above. Not all of them, three. Enough to give you a real picture, few enough that you'll actually keep tracking them.

Check them on a schedule rather than obsessively. Monthly works well for measurements and photos, and lab work runs on whatever schedule your team sets.

Write down your non-scale wins as they happen, because you will forget them. Memory is heavily biased toward the scale, since the scale produces a number and the other wins produce a feeling. A running list is how you keep a bad weigh-in from erasing eight months of genuine progress.

And review the list specifically during plateaus. That's what it's for. A plateau on weight while strength climbs and clothes loosen is not a plateau in any meaningful sense. It's just one metric being quiet.

Maintenance Is Not the Consolation Prize

There's a cultural script that says weight loss is the achievement and maintenance is what happens after the interesting part is over. That script is backwards.

Losing weight is something an enormous number of people have done successfully, often several times, us included. Keeping it off is the part that's genuinely hard and genuinely rare. If you're maintaining, you are not stalled. You are doing the difficult thing.

This isn't a call to throw out numbers entirely. Tracking still matters, and we've said so throughout this book. It's a call to stop treating the scale as the only judge in the room. Let it be one data point among several, not the entire verdict.

The turtle-slow, invisible months are often exactly when the most durable progress is happening. The kind that doesn't show up fast and also doesn't disappear fast, which is a trade most of us would take every time if we actually understood that's what was on offer.

Chapter 28: For the Guys, From Marcio

Renata got her chapter, so the guys get one too, and I'll open with the confession that explains half my decisions in this book: I faint at blood. Not squeamish. Faint. Full lights-out, vasovagal, catch-me-before-the-floor-does faint. I am six foot four and I have lost consciousness at medical procedures that a child would sit through with a lollipop. If you're a man who has been quietly avoiding doctors, needles, or the entire concept of surgery because of some version of this, I am your people, and I still got it done. Arrange the logistics around your weakness instead of pretending it doesn't exist. I literally scheduled my surgery first so I wouldn't see my wife's stitches and chicken out. That's not weakness management, that's engineering.

Now the things guys actually ask each other in the group chats, answered honestly.

The gym is not optional for us, and I don't mean that as a discipline lecture. Men lose muscle alongside fat during rapid weight loss, and if you don't fight to keep it, you end up a smaller, softer version of yourself instead of a stronger one. Body recomposition, trading fat for muscle at the same weight, became my entire second year, and it is slow, invisible, dinner-party-boring work. It is also the reason my 18 year old called me a gym rat, which remains the single best compliment of my adult life.

Your posture is going to need actual work. Your muscles spent years being scaffolding for weight that's no longer there, and when it leaves, your center of gravity relocates without telling you. I needed functional range therapy and targeted exercises just to learn how to stand like the new frame I'd earned. Budget for that, it's part of the project, not an add-on.

Chapter 29: Building Your Support System

We've spent most of this book talking about the two of us, because doing this together was genuinely the biggest factor in why it worked. But we want to be honest about something: not everyone reading this has a partner who's doing it alongside them, and if that's you, we don't want this book to have accidentally implied you need one to succeed. You don't. You need support. It just doesn't have to come from exactly the same place ours did.

The Four Kinds of Support, and Why You Need More Than One

People tend to look for one person to be everything, which is both unrealistic and unfair to that person. It's more useful to think in categories.

Medical support is your care team. Surgeon, dietitian, primary doctor, mental health provider. They handle the questions that have correct answers.

Peer support is other people who have been through it. This is the only category that can offer recognition instead of explanation. There is a specific relief in describing dumping syndrome, hair loss, or a stall to someone who doesn't need any of it defined first.

Daily-life support is whoever shares your kitchen, your schedule, and your grocery list. This is the category with the most practical influence over whether your rhythm works day to day.

Emotional support is the person who gets the honest version, including the bad weeks. Sometimes this is a spouse, sometimes a friend, sometimes a therapist, sometimes someone from a group who has never met you in person.

One person occasionally covers several of these. Nobody covers all four well. Mapping which ones you have and which ones are thin is more productive than a general feeling of being unsupported.

Who Actually Showed Up For Us

Our surgical team, for the medical side. A handful of people who noticed changes and said so out loud, sometimes a coworker, sometimes a stranger, once, memorably, our own teenage daughter, who had absolutely no idea how much three casual words would matter. And the bariatric community itself, even in the indirect way of simply knowing other people had walked this exact road and lived to joke about the hair loss and the dumping syndrome myths right alongside us.

The two of us covered daily-life and emotional support for each other, which is a genuine advantage and also, we'd point out, a single point of failure. If we'd both hit a bad stretch simultaneously, we'd have had nobody outside the house holding a rope. That's an argument for building outside connections even when your household support is strong.

If You're Doing This Alone

Here's where real support tends to live when a partner isn't part of the picture.

Bariatric-specific support groups, in person or online, where the very specific, very weird details of this experience don't need to be explained from scratch to someone who's never heard of a pouch. Look for groups organized around your phase, since a group that's entirely pre-op has different needs than one at year three.

One deliberately chosen person. Not everyone. One or two people you trust enough to be honest with about the hard weeks, not just the wins. Tell them explicitly what you need, because most people genuinely don't know. "I'd like you to ask me how it's going once a month, and I'd like you not to comment on my food" is a real, answerable request. "Be supportive" isn't.

Your care team, used as more than an appointment to survive. They can catch problems early if you actually tell them what's happening. Many programs include dietitian or counseling visits that go unclaimed for years.

A therapist, particularly one who works with eating, body image, or health behavior change. This is support, not a sign that something has gone wrong.

When People Aren't Supportive

This is common enough that leaving it out would be dishonest.

The food pusher. Frequently well-meaning and culturally driven, especially in families like ours where feeding people is how affection gets expressed. A short, warm, repeatable line works better than an explanation: "It looks amazing, I'm just eating small these days." Repeat it verbatim. Do not escalate. Most food pushing dies from boredom, not from argument.

The person who calls it cheating. Worth deciding in advance that you're not going to litigate this. Surgery is a tool that requires years of work to keep functioning, which anyone who has read this far already knows. You are not obligated to prove that to a coworker.

The person who's threatened by the change. This one is real and it's painful, and it usually has nothing to do with you. Weight loss can disturb the dynamics of a friendship or a marriage, particularly where shared eating habits were part of the bond. Sometimes it resolves. Sometimes it needs a direct conversation. Occasionally it changes the relationship permanently.

The commentary on your body. Both directions are exhausting. "You're getting too thin" and "you look so much better" can both land badly. A neutral deflection and a subject change is a complete response. You do not owe anyone your numbers.

You are also allowed to simply not discuss it. Privacy is a legitimate strategy, not avoidance.

Being Someone's Support

If you're reading this because someone you love is going through it, the most useful things you can do are smaller than you'd think.

Ask how it's going and then actually listen without offering a solution. Don't comment on their plate, in either direction. Don't announce their surgery to people on their behalf. Do learn enough about the mechanics to not need everything explained repeatedly. Do celebrate non-scale wins, which they're likely undercounting themselves. And handle your own feelings about their change somewhere that isn't their lap.

The Through-Line

Whether it's a spouse, a friend, or a group of strangers on the internet who happen to understand exactly what a plateau feels like, the point is the same one we keep coming back to: this works better witnessed than done in total isolation.

Find your version of a witness. It doesn't have to look like ours to count.

Quick Reference: When to Call Your Doctor, Not Your Feed

This list is not exhaustive, and it is not a diagnostic tool. It's a reminder that certain things belong in a phone call to your care team, not a Google search or a support group post, regardless of what phase you're in.

Call your team, promptly, if you notice:

  • Sharp or severe pain that's new, worsening, or doesn't resolve with rest

  • Persistent vomiting or an inability to keep food or fluids down

  • Signs of dehydration, dizziness, very dark urine, confusion

  • A weight change, in either direction, that's rapid and doesn't match your recent habits

  • Any symptom that's genuinely scaring you, even if you can't articulate exactly why

None of these belong in a "let's wait and see" category. When in doubt, the call costs you five minutes. Not calling can cost you a lot more.
